# PROVERB 22 — A Good Name Over Great Riches

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Proverbs 22:1–29. Two sections meet here: 22:1–16 closes Solomon's sayings (10:1–22:16), and 22:17 opens the "Thirty Sayings" (22:17–24:22) — a numbered collection with close parallels to the Egyptian Instruction of Amenemope. Clustered by theme (every verse present): reputation and wealth, the poor and the lender, the child (22:6, 22:15 — second rod verse), the Thirty Sayings' first eight, and the yirah verse 22:4. Companion: Prov 13:24; 19:18; 3:5–6; Ps 15.*

---

Chapter twenty-two is a seam. Halfway through, the voice changes — Solomon's two-liners end, and a new teacher starts numbering his sayings. Read the first half as the last of the stones, and the second half as the opening of a new bag.

**"A good name is to be chosen over great riches; favor is better than silver and gold."**

Nivchar shem — a name is to be chosen. The Hebrew just says shem, "name" — the "good" is implied. Your name is the one asset that follows you into the room before you get there and stays after you leave. Chapter ten said the name of the wicked rots. Chapter eighteen said the Name is a tower. Choose yours over the money. You'll need it longer.

First handful — rich and poor. Verse two: **"The rich and the poor meet together; God is the Maker of them all."** Nifgashu — they run into each other. Same street, same Maker. Whatever the difference in their bank accounts, the difference in their origin is zero. Chapter fourteen and seventeen said grinding the poor insults the Maker — here's why: same Craftsman.

Verse seven: **"The rich rules over the poor, and the borrower is servant to the lender."** Eved — slave. That's not approval; that's the mechanism. Debt is a leash. Verse sixteen: **"Whoever oppresses the poor to increase his own wealth, or gives to the rich — both come to poverty."** Verse twenty-two, in the new section: "Don't rob the poor because he's poor, and don't crush the afflicted at the gate." Ki dal hu — because he's poor. Don't take from him precisely because he can't fight back. That's the definition of predation, and the book names it. Verse twenty-three: **"For God will plead their case and rob the life of those who robbed them."** Yariv rivam — He takes their lawsuit. The poor man has counsel. It's God. Verse nine: **"The one with a generous eye will be blessed, for he gives his bread to the poor."** Tov-ayin — good of eye. The opposite of chapter twenty-three's evil eye. Generosity is a way of seeing. Verse twenty-six and twenty-seven, the co-signing warning one last time: "Don't be one of those who shake hands and put up security for debts. If you have nothing to pay, why should your bed be taken from under you?" Fifth time. They'll take the bed.

Second handful — the mouth and the heart. Verse eleven: **"He who loves purity of heart and has gracious lips — the king is his friend."** T'hor-lev — clean of heart, Psalm 24's word. Verse ten: **"Drive out the mocker and strife goes out; quarrels and insults cease."** Garesh letz — expel him. Sometimes the fix for a room is one person leaving it. Verse twelve: **"The eyes of God guard knowledge, but He overthrows the words of the treacherous."** Verse fourteen: **"The mouth of the strange woman is a deep pit; the one God is angry with falls in."** Shuchah amuqqah — chapter seven's stranger, now as a pit. The pit-digger, from the other side. Verse eight: **"Whoever sows injustice reaps trouble, and the rod of his fury will fail."** You reap what you sow — Paul in Galatians got it here — and shevet evrato, the rod of his rage, yichleh, is finished. The man who rules by anger runs out of rod. Verse thirteen: **"The sluggard says, 'There's a lion outside! I'll be killed in the streets!'"** He invents a lion. Chapter twenty-six will have him say it again. Every excuse is a lion he made up.

Third handful — the child. Two verses, and they're the most quoted parenting lines in Scripture.

Verse six: **"Train up a child according to his way, and even when he's old he won't turn from it."**

Chanoch la-na'ar al-pi darko. Everyone knows "train up a child in the way he should go." But al-pi darko is "according to his way" — the child's own way, his bent, his grain. The Hebrew doesn't say "the right way." It says his way. Which means the verse is either a promise — learn how this kid is wired and shape him along that grain, and it holds — or a warning — let a kid do whatever he wants and that holds too. Both readings are honest to the Hebrew. And both say the same thing to a parent: pay attention to the actual child. Not the child you planned. The one in front of you. And chanoch — that's the word for *dedicating* a house or a temple. You're not programming him. You're dedicating him. Consecrating a life.

Verse fifteen: **"Folly is bound up in the heart of a child, but the rod of training drives it far from him."**

Here's the second rod verse. Chapter thirteen gave you the shepherd's rod — Psalm 23's, the one that comforts. Here it's shevet musar — the rod of training, coaching's rod, the shepherd's job. And notice what it says folly is: q'shurah — tied, knotted into the kid's heart. Not evil — folly, the naive-and-thick-headed kind, chapter one's ivvelet. Every child comes with it knotted in. And the shepherd's job is yarchiqennah — to move it far away. Untie the knot; put distance between the kid and his own foolishness. That's a long verb. It doesn't happen in one blow. It happens over years of showing up early, chapter thirteen. Read verse six and fifteen together: know the child's grain; steadily separate him from his folly. Neither one says hit. And chapter nineteen already told you where the line is — never let training become destruction.

The yirah verse. Verse four:

**"The reward for humility is the AWE of God — riches, honor, and life."**

Or, the more common reading: eqev anavah yirat YHWH — "the result of humility and awe of God is riches, honor, and life." Either way the Hebrew glues anavah and yirah together — humility and awe are one thing, or one produces the other. Chapter fifteen: awe is the training; before honor, humility. Chapter eighteen: before honor, humility. Now: humility plus awe yields honor. And put "fear" in there — "the reward for humility is the fear of the LORD." Being humble gets you terror? No. Being small before something great is what awe is. Humility is awe from the inside; awe is humility looking up. Same thing. Three payoffs: riches, honor, chayyim. Life. Again.

Now the seam. Verse seventeen: **"Incline your ear and hear the words of the wise; apply your heart to my knowledge. For it's pleasant if you keep them within you, if all of them are ready on your lips."**

New teacher. New collection — the old Bibles miss it, but the Hebrew of verse twenty says shalishim — "thirty" — "have I not written you thirty sayings?" Most translations render it "excellent things," but the letters spell thirty, and if you count the units from here to 24:22, you get thirty. This is a numbered course. And it looks a lot like an Egyptian one — the Instruction of Amenemope, written centuries before Solomon, has the same sequence: honest boundaries, don't rob the poor, don't eat with the stingy, don't befriend the hot-tempered. Israel's wise men read the neighbors' wisdom and baptized it — kept what was true, put God's name on it. That's not embarrassing. That's what wisdom does. Truth is truth wherever you dig it up.

Verse nineteen: **"So your trust may be in God — I've made them known to you today, even to you."** Af-attah — even you. He's looking at one person. Verse twenty-one: **"To make you know truth, reliable words, so you can bring back a true report to those who sent you."** Whoever sent you — your family, your boss, your God — you're supposed to come back with the truth. Saying two, verse twenty-four and twenty-five: **"Don't befriend a hot-tempered man; don't go with a wrathful man, or you'll learn his ways and get your soul snared."** Ba'al af — master of nostril. You catch anger from people. Chapter thirteen — choose the room. Saying three, twenty-six and twenty-seven — the bed. Saying four, twenty-eight: "Don't move the ancient boundary stone your fathers set up." Amenemope has this one almost word for word. Property lines. Chapter fifteen — God defends the widow's boundary. Don't be the one who nudges the fence overnight.

And saying five, verse twenty-nine, the last line: "Do you see a man skilled in his work? He'll stand before kings; he won't stand before obscure men."

Ish mahir bi-mlachto — a man quick, skilled at his craft. Not a rich man. Not a well-connected one. A man who's good at the thing. That man ends up in rooms he couldn't have bought his way into. Skill is its own introduction. Bezalel built the tabernacle because he could work metal. Get so good they can't not call you.

Here's the shape. Choose a name over riches. Rich and poor have the same Maker — don't rob a man because he can't fight back; God takes his case. Know your child's actual grain and steadily move his folly far off — no verse says hit. Humility and awe are one thing, and it pays life. Then a new teacher with thirty numbered sayings, some borrowed from Egypt and baptized. Don't catch anger from the angry. Don't move the fence. And get so skilled they call you into the king's room.

Today: pick the one skill you'd stand before kings with, and put an hour into it. Then look at the child — yours or someone's — and ask what their actual grain is, not the one you drew up.

*Note on translation: 22:4 eqev anavah yirat YHWH — "by humility and the fear of the LORD are riches" (KJV) — rendered with yirah = awe; the syntax either equates humility with awe or makes awe the reward of humility — both readings unify the two; cf. 15:33, 18:12. 22:1 shem alone — "good" is supplied. 22:2 nifgashu = meet/encounter; oseh chullam = Maker of them all (14:31, 17:5). 22:6 chanoch la-na'ar al-pi darko — chanoch = dedicate (Deut 20:5, 1 Kgs 8:63); al-pi darko = according to HIS way — the "way he should go" of KJV is interpretive; both the promise-reading and the warning-reading are noted; the verse is absent from the LXX. 22:8 shevet evrato yichleh = the rod of his wrath fails — a rod that runs out; zorea avlah yiqtzor-aven → Gal 6:7. 22:9 tov-ayin = good of eye (contrast 23:6, 28:22 ra-ayin). 22:13 sluggard's lion = 26:13. 22:14 shuchah amuqqah = deep pit. 22:15 ivvelet q'shurah v'lev-na'ar = folly is bound/knotted in the child's heart; shevet musar = rod of training — the shepherd's rod (13:24 note); yarchiqennah = drives it FAR from him — durative; control texts 17:10, 19:18. 22:17 begins the Thirty Sayings; 22:20 Ketiv shilshom ("formerly"), Qere shalishim ("officers/excellent things") — emended by most modern scholars to sh'loshim = "thirty," matching the count of units to 24:22 and the thirty-chapter Instruction of Amenemope (c. 1300–1075 BCE), whose parallels include 22:22 (ch. 2), 22:24–25 (ch. 9), 22:28 (ch. 6), 22:29 (ch. 30), 23:1–3, 23:4–5, 23:6–8, 23:10–11. 22:22 ki dal hu = because he is poor. 22:23 yariv rivam = pleads their plea. 22:24 ba'al af = master of nostril/anger. 22:26 co-signing (6:1, 11:15, 17:18, 20:16). 22:29 ish mahir bi-mlachto = a man skilled/swift in his work. Yirah in this chapter: 22:4.*
