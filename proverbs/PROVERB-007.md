# PROVERB 7 — The Ox Didn't Know

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Proverbs 7:1–27. The fourth and final strange-woman lecture — told as a story the father watched from his window: a young man at twilight, a woman dressed for the hunt, her whole speech word for word, and the ox walking to slaughter. Companion: Prov 5 and 6:20–35; the anti-portrait of Lady Wisdom in ch. 8.*

---

The father has warned you three times. Now he stops lecturing and tells you a story. He watched it happen.

But first, the setup, verses one through five: **"My son, keep my words; store up my commands inside you. Keep my commands and live; guard my teaching like the pupil of your eye. Tie them on your fingers; write them on the tablet of your heart. Say to wisdom, 'You are my sister,' and call understanding your relative — to keep you from the strange woman, from the foreigner with her smooth words."**

Hear the move. Call wisdom your sister. Make her family. Because here's the truth the father knows: you're going to be in a relationship with some woman's voice in your head. Either Lady Wisdom's or the stranger's. He's saying — get the right one in the house first. Fill the seat before the wrong one takes it.

Then verse six, and the camera turns on. "At the window of my house, through my lattice, I looked down — and I saw among the naive, I noticed among the young men, a young man with no sense —" chasar lev — heart-lacking. Not evil. Empty. The control room from 4:23 has nobody in it.

**"— crossing the street near her corner, walking toward her house, at twilight, as the day faded, in the dark of night."**

Notice three time-markers in one verse. Twilight. Evening. Night. The father is telling you: this kid chose the hour. Nobody wanders past that corner at dusk by accident. The decision happened before he left home. By the time he's on her street, it's a formality.

Verse ten: **"And look — a woman comes to meet him, dressed like a prostitute, with a guarded heart."** N'tzurat lev. Her heart is guarded — the same word as 4:23, "guard your heart" — but inverted. She's guarded the wrong thing. She's protected her intentions, not her wellspring. The kid has no heart; she has a locked one. That's the pair.

Verses eleven and twelve: **"She's loud and rebellious; her feet don't stay home. Now in the street, now in the plazas, at every corner she lies in wait."** She's Lady Wisdom's shadow. Wisdom stood in the street and the plazas and shouted in chapter one — same words, same places. Now here's the counterfeit, in the same spot, using the same volume. That's the terrifying thing. They stand on the same corner. Only the message is different.

Verse thirteen: **"She grabs him and kisses him and, with a brazen face, says to him —"**

And now the father gives you her whole pitch. Every line. Memorize it, because it's still the pitch.

"I had to offer peace-offerings today; today I paid my vows." Line one: religion. She opens with church. She's just come from the temple. She's a good person. She's been giving. This is how it starts — with respectability. The affair always begins in a context where nobody would suspect.

"So I came out to meet you, to look for your face — and I found you." Line two: destiny. It was meant to be. I was looking for you specifically. Of all the men in the city. You're special. The kid who's chasar lev — heart-empty — has never been told he was special. This lands.

"I've spread my couch with coverings, colored linens from Egypt. I've perfumed my bed with myrrh, aloes, and cinnamon." Line three: sensory overload. Egypt linen — the imported stuff. Three spices. She's describing the room so he can smell it before he's in it. Whatever the modern version is — she's built the whole scene and now she's just narrating it.

"Come, let's drink our fill of love until morning; let's delight ourselves with lovemaking." Line four: no consequences. All night. Just pleasure. She uses the word dodim — the Song of Songs word for love. She's borrowing the marriage-bed vocabulary for the theft.

"Because my husband isn't home. He's gone on a long journey. He took a bag of money with him; he won't be back until the full moon." Line five, and it's the closer: the coast is clear. Nobody will know. He's far. He's got cash — he's on business, he's not coming back early. There's a calendar for this. She's done the math. And notice — she says "the man," ha-ish. Not "my husband." She won't even give him a title. She's already erased him in her mouth.

Verse twenty-one: **"With her persuasive words she turns him; with the smoothness of her lips she drives him."** Tadichennu — she pushes him. He doesn't walk in. He's herded in.

And then verse twenty-two, the line the whole chapter exists for: "Suddenly he follows her — like an ox goes to the slaughter, like a deer stepping into a noose — until an arrow splits his liver. Like a bird rushing into the snare, he doesn't know it will cost him his life."

The ox doesn't know. That's it. That's the whole tragedy. The ox walks calmly to the slaughterhouse because to the ox, it looks like a walk. The kid follows her because to him, it looks like the best night of his life. V'lo yada — and he doesn't know. Chapter one said the bird that sees the net escapes. This bird didn't see it. And the father, at the window, sees the whole thing — the corner, the hour, the speech, the noose — and the boy sees none of it.

That's why he told the story. Not to scare you. To give you the window. So next time you're the one on the street at twilight, some part of you is also upstairs, watching, and it can say: I know how this ends. I've seen this before.

Verses twenty-four through twenty-seven, the close: "So now, sons, listen to me; pay attention to the words of my mouth. Don't let your heart turn aside to her ways; don't stray onto her paths. Because many are the victims she's brought down; numerous are all her slain. Her house is the road to Sheol, going down to the chambers of death."

Not hell. The grave. Chadrei mavet — the rooms of death. Her bedroom, the one with the Egyptian linen and the three spices, has a back door, and the back door opens into the grave. Same as chapter two, same as chapter five. Not fire. Silence. Something in you that doesn't come back.

Here's the shape. Make wisdom your sister before the stranger makes herself your lover — the seat will be filled by somebody. The kid chose the hour before he chose the street. She stands on Wisdom's own corner, using Wisdom's own volume. Her pitch is religion, destiny, sensation, no-consequences, and the-coast-is-clear — and it's still the pitch. And the ox didn't know.

Today: put yourself at the window. Whatever your twilight street is — the one you tell yourself you're just walking past — look down at yourself from upstairs. You know how it ends. Turn around before the corner.

---

*Note on translation: 7:2 k'ishon einecha = like the pupil (lit. "little man") of your eye — the apple-of-the-eye idiom. 7:4 achoti at = "you are my sister" — kinship language; in Song of Songs "my sister, my bride" is the beloved, so wisdom is being offered the intimate seat. 7:7 chasar lev = lacking heart/sense — the empty control room (cf. 4:23). 7:10 n'tzurat lev — "subtle of heart" (KJV) — literally "guarded of heart," the same root as 4:23 n'tzor libbecha; the inversion is deliberate. 7:11–12 ba-chutz / ba-r'chovot = in the street / in the plazas — verbatim the locations of Lady Wisdom in 1:20–21. 7:14 zivchei sh'lamim = peace-offerings; her opening line is religious. 7:16 etun Mitzrayim = Egyptian linen. 7:18 dodim = lovemaking (Song of Songs vocabulary). 7:19 ha-ish — "the man," not ishi "my husband" — the erasure is in the Hebrew. 7:21 tadichennu = drives/pushes him. 7:22 the deer/noose clause is textually difficult (MT k'eches el-musar evil, "like an anklet to the correction of a fool"); the deer/stag reading follows LXX/Syriac and is the majority modern reconstruction — both noted. 7:22–23 v'lo yada = and he does not know. 7:27 Sheol / chadrei mavet — "hell" in KJV — rendered the grave / chambers of death. No yirah in this chapter.*
