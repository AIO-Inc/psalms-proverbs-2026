# PROVERB 31 — A Woman Who Stands in Awe

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Proverbs 31:1–31. The last chapter, in two parts: the words of King Lemuel's mother (31:1–9) — another Massa outsider, and the only teaching in the book explicitly from a woman — and the eshet chayil acrostic (31:10–31): twenty-two verses, one per Hebrew letter, aleph to tav, portraying the Woman of Strength. Verse 30 carries the book's last yirah — the closing bookend to 1:7. Companion: Prov 1:7; 9:1–5; 12:4; 20:6; Ruth 3:11; Ps 93:1, 104:1; Ps 111→112.*

---

Thirty chapters. The father spoke, then Solomon's stones, then a new teacher with thirty sayings, then Hezekiah's scribes, then an exhausted outsider who said he was too dumb to be a man. And now — the last two voices in Proverbs are both women. A mother teaching her son. And a portrait of a woman that the book saves for its final word, and gives the whole alphabet to.

Part one — Lemuel's mother. Verse one: **"The words of King Lemuel of Massa, which his mother taught him."**

Asher-yiss'rattu immo. The musar verb — his mother coached him. Same word as every "training" in the book. Chapter one said don't abandon your mother's teaching; chapter six said the same; chapter twenty-three said don't despise her when she's old. Here's what she said.

Verse two: **"What, my son? What, son of my womb? What, son of my vows?"** Mah b'ri — the Aramaic bar, "son" — three times, like a mother who can't quite believe he's grown enough to need this talk. Bar-n'darai — son of my vows. Hannah's language — she promised this boy to God before he was born. That's the voice: a mother who prayed for him and now has to warn him.

Verse three: **"Don't give your strength to women, or your ways to those who destroy kings."** Chapter five, seven — the stranger, now from the mother's mouth. She's not against women; she is one. She's against the kind of entanglement that has ended dynasties, and she's seen it.

Verses four through seven: **"It's not for kings, Lemuel — not for kings to drink wine, nor for rulers to crave strong drink — or they'll drink and forget what's been decreed, and pervert the rights of all the afflicted. Give strong drink to the one who's perishing, and wine to the bitter of soul; let him drink and forget his poverty and remember his misery no more."** Chapter twenty, twenty-three — wine is a mocker. But the mother's angle is different: a king can't drink because a king has to remember the law and judge the poor. And then — a mercy: the dying man, the bitter — let him drink. She's not a prohibitionist. She's saying: the one who holds power over others doesn't get to be numb.

Verses eight and nine, her last words, and they're the reason her son was a king worth remembering:

**"Open your mouth for the mute, for the rights of all who are left desolate. Open your mouth, judge rightly, and defend the rights of the poor and needy."**

P'tach-picha l'illem. Open your mouth for the one who can't speak. Twice — p'tach-picha — open your mouth. Chapter twenty-four — rescue the ones being dragged. Chapter twenty-nine — the throne lasts as long as it judges the poor faithfully. The mother distills the entire political teaching of the book into one instruction: you have a voice; use it for the ones who don't. That's what a mother who made vows over a baby tells him when he's a king.

Part two — the Woman of Strength. Verse ten, and the book starts counting the alphabet.

**"A woman of strength — who can find? Her worth is far beyond rubies."**

Eshet chayil mi yimtza. Three things.

First, the word. Your Bible says "virtuous woman." Chayil is not "virtuous." Chayil is valor. Might. It's what you call a soldier — gibbor chayil, a mighty warrior. It's the word Boaz used for Ruth — "all my people know you are an eshet chayil." A woman of strength. A woman of force. Chapter twelve said she's her husband's crown. The English softened her into a lady with good manners. The Hebrew put her in armor.

Second, the question. Mi yimtza — who can find? Chapter twenty, verse six: **"a faithful man — who can find?"** Same words. The book asked it about a man and left it hanging. Now it asks about a woman — and answers it, for twenty-two verses. The faithful man was rare and undescribed. The woman of strength gets the whole alphabet.

Third, the alphabet. Verse ten starts with aleph. Verse eleven, bet. Verse twelve, gimel. All the way to verse thirty-one, tav. Twenty-two letters, twenty-two verses. Psalm 119 did this — Psalm 111 and 112 did this. It means completeness. A to Z. The scribes are saying: here is a whole life, nothing left out, every letter used. And they gave that structure — the most careful, most complete form they had — to her.

Now — who is she? Two answers, and both are right. One: she's a real woman, and Jewish husbands have sung this passage to their wives every Friday night at the Sabbath table for centuries. Eshet Chayil. It's a love song. Two: she's Lady Wisdom — the woman who built her house in chapter nine, set her table, called from the corners in chapter one and eight — come home. Wisdom stopped being a voice on the street and became a woman at a real table, with real servants and real wool. The book that opened with Wisdom as an idea closes with Wisdom as a person you could marry. Hold both. The scribes meant both.

Let's walk the alphabet.

Bet, verse eleven: **"Her husband's heart trusts in her, and he lacks no gain."** Batach bah lev ba'lah — he trusts her. The book said don't trust your own heart, chapter twenty-eight; trust God, sixteen. Here's the one human it says a heart can rest on. Gimel, verse twelve: **"She does him good and not harm all the days of her life."** Dalet, verse thirteen: **"She seeks out wool and flax and works with willing hands."** B'chefetz kappeha — her palms delight in the work. She's not grinding. She likes it. He, verse fourteen: **"She is like the merchant ships, bringing her food from afar."** Ships. She's commerce. She's logistics.

Vav, verse fifteen: **"She rises while it is still night and gives food to her household, and a portion to her servant girls."** B'od laylah — while it's still night. Psalm 57 — I'll wake the dawn. Psalm 30 — weeping stays the night; joy in the morning. She's up before the sun and she's feeding people. And choq l'na'aroteha — the ration, Agur's word from 30:8, for the servant girls. Chapter twenty-seven — life for the maidservants. She's the shepherd of the flock, and the lowest in the house eats first.

Zayin, verse sixteen: **"She considers a field and buys it; from her earnings she plants a vineyard."** Zam'mah sadeh — she sizes up a field. She's in real estate. She evaluates and buys. This is a woman with capital and judgment about how to use it, three thousand years ago. Chet, verse seventeen: **"She girds her loins with strength and makes her arms strong."** Chag'rah v'oz motneha — she belts herself with power. The warrior word again. T'ammetz z'ro'oteha — she strengthens her arms. This is physical. She lifts.

Tet, verse eighteen: **"She perceives that her merchandise is good; her lamp doesn't go out at night."** Chapter thirteen, twenty, twenty-four — the lamp of the wicked goes out. Hers doesn't. She's the lit lamp, all night. Yod, verse nineteen: **"She puts her hands to the distaff, and her palms grasp the spindle."** Craft. Actual skill in the hands, chapter twenty-two's man who stands before kings.

Kaf, verse twenty: **"She opens her palm to the poor and stretches out her hands to the needy."** Kappah par'sah le-ani. Her palm — same word as verse thirteen's delighting palms and verse nineteen's spinning palms — opened to the poor. The hand that works is the hand that gives. Chapter fourteen, seventeen — grinding the poor insults the Maker. Chapter nineteen — giving to the poor lends to God. Chapter twenty-eight — hiding your eyes brings curses. She doesn't hide her eyes. She opens her palm. The mother said "open your mouth for the mute"; the woman opens her hand for the poor. Two openings. That's the whole ethic of the book, in a mother and a wife.

Lamed, verse twenty-one: **"She isn't afraid of snow for her household, for all of them are clothed in scarlet."** Lo-tira — she does not fear. Real fear, yare in its plain human sense — and she doesn't have it, about winter, because she prepared. The ant, chapter six. Awe of God, yes — verse thirty — but dread of the snow? No. She stocked the closet. Mem, verse twenty-two: **"She makes coverings for herself; her clothing is fine linen and purple."** Shesh v'argaman — linen and purple. Royal cloth. She's not dowdy. She dresses like the queen she is. Nun, verse twenty-three: **"Her husband is known in the gates, when he sits among the elders of the land."** He's respected — because of her. Chapter twelve — a woman of strength is her husband's crown. Here's the crown at the city gate, and everybody knows who made it. Samech, verse twenty-four: **"She makes linen garments and sells them, and supplies sashes to the merchants."** Export. She's got a supply chain.

Ayin, verse twenty-five:

**"Strength and majesty are her clothing, and she laughs at the days to come."**

Oz-v'hadar l'vushah. Strength and majesty — hadar. Those aren't just nice words. Psalm 93 — "God reigns, He is clothed in majesty." Psalm 104 — "You are clothed with splendor and majesty." Oz v'hadar is what God wears. The scribes put God's own wardrobe on a human woman. And then — va-tischaq l'yom acharon — she laughs at the last day. Chapter eight — Wisdom playing before God at creation, m'sacheqet, same root. Chapter twenty-seven — don't boast about tomorrow, you don't know what it'll birth. She's not boasting. She's laughing — because she's prepared for the snow, and because acharit, the future, chapter twenty-three and twenty-four — she has one. That's what awe looks like on a face. Not terror. Laughter at tomorrow.

Pe, verse twenty-six: **"She opens her mouth with wisdom, and the teaching of loyal-love is on her tongue."**

Torat-chesed al-l'shonah. The torah — the instruction — of chesed — loyal-love — is on her tongue. Chapter three — bind loyal-love and truth around your neck. Chapter sixteen — by chesed wrong is covered. She's got it in her mouth. When she talks, what comes out is the teaching of covenant faithfulness. Chapter ten said the mouth is the tell; here's what hers tells. And — pat'chah piha — she opens her mouth. The mother said "open your mouth for the mute." The woman opens hers with wisdom — the same verb, the third opening.

Tzade, verse twenty-seven: **"She watches over the ways of her household and doesn't eat the bread of idleness."** Tzofiyah — she keeps watch — the watchman word, chapter fifteen: "the eyes of God are in every place, keeping watch." She has God's eyes on the house. And lechem atzlut — the bread of sluggardness — she doesn't touch it. The sluggard's whole chapter twenty-six, negated in one line.

Qof, verse twenty-eight: **"Her children rise up and call her happy; her husband also, and he praises her."** Va-y'ash'ruha — they call her ashrei. The Psalm 1 word. The first word of the Psalter. Happy, flourishing, on the right track. Her kids stand up and say it. Resh, verse twenty-nine, and it's his line: "'Many women have done valiantly, but you surpass them all.'" Asu chayil — done chayil, done valor. The husband, at the Sabbath table, to his wife: a lot of women have been strong. You're the strongest.

Shin, verse thirty. This is the one. This is the last yirah in the book, and the whole book has been walking toward it.

**"Charm is deceptive and beauty is a breath — but a woman who stands in AWE of God, she is to be praised."**

Read your Bible's version: "a woman that feareth the LORD, she shall be praised." Now put thirty-one chapters behind it. Chapter one: awe is the beginning of knowledge. Chapter two: awe is what you dig your way to. Chapter three: awe heals your bones. Chapter eight: awe is hating evil and can't sit in a room with pride. Chapter nine: awe is the beginning of wisdom. Chapter ten: awe adds days. Chapter fourteen: awe is a fortress your children inherit, and a fountain. Chapter fifteen: awe is the training, and before honor comes humility. Chapter sixteen: God's loyal-love covers the wrong, and then awe turns you. Chapter nineteen: awe is a pillow — you sleep satisfied. Chapter twenty-two: humility and awe are one thing and they pay life. Chapter twenty-three: stay in awe all day and you'll have a future. And every time the book meant real fear — dread of harm, dread of a king, dread of people, dread of your own slip — it used a different word, and we kept it as fear, so you could hear the difference.

Yir'at-YHWH. Awe. And here she is: the woman who has it. Hi tit'hallal — she — emphatic — she is the one to be praised. Not the charming one. Sheqer ha-chen — charm is a lie. Not the beautiful one. Hevel ha-yofi — beauty is hevel, Ecclesiastes' vapor, Psalm 39's four-fingers-wide breath. Those are real and they're gone by morning. What lasts — what's praiseworthy — is a human being who stands in awe of God. Who's been made small by something great and got bigger for it. Who laughs at tomorrow because she knows Who holds it. Psalm 111 said awe is the beginning of wisdom, and then Psalm 112 painted the man who had it. Proverbs 1:7 said awe is the beginning of knowledge, and then Proverbs 31 painted the woman who has it. Both times — the awe-person turns out to be gracious, generous, unshaken, open-handed to the poor, laughing at the future. That's the portrait. Not a cowering servant. A queen in linen and purple with God's own majesty on her shoulders and a spindle in her hand, up before dawn feeding the maidservants.

If the word had been "fear" all along — if the book were really about being scared of God — this woman would make no sense. She isn't afraid of the snow. She laughs at the last day. Fear doesn't produce her. Awe does. She is the proof, at the end of the book, that the translation was wrong and the Hebrew was right.

Tav, verse thirty-one, the last letter, the last line: "Give her the fruit of her hands, and let her works praise her in the gates."

T'nu-lah mi-p'ri yadeha. Give her — imperative — the fruit of her hands. Chapter twelve, thirteen, eighteen — you eat the fruit of your own mouth, your own deeds. The book's whole justice system — you get what you sowed — ends with a command to make sure she gets hers. And y'hal'luha va-sh'arim ma'aseha — her works praise her in the gates. Not her husband, this time. Not her kids. Her works. The things she did stand up in the public square and testify. Chapter twenty-two, verse twenty-nine — the skilled worker stands before kings. She doesn't even have to show up. Her work stands there for her.

Here's the shape of the last chapter. A mother who made vows over a baby tells the king: don't get numb, and open your mouth for the mute. Then twenty-two letters for a woman of strength — the soldier word — who sizes up fields, belts herself with power, rises in the dark to feed the lowest in the house, opens her palm to the poor, wears God's own strength and majesty, laughs at tomorrow, and speaks the teaching of loyal-love. And at the second-to-last letter, the book's final yirah: charm lies and beauty evaporates, but the one who stands in awe of God — she is to be praised. Give her the fruit of her hands.

And here's the shape of the whole book. It opened with a father at a kitchen table saying awe is where it starts. It closes with a woman at a Sabbath table who is what awe looks like when it grows up. Wisdom shouted on the corner in chapter one; in chapter thirty-one she's home, and the lamp's still lit. Today — and this is the last "today" of the series: find your eshet chayil, or the person in your life who's closest to her — man or woman, wife or mother or friend — the one who's up before dawn feeding people and laughing at the future. Give them the fruit of their hands. Say it out loud. And then go be someone who stands in awe — not afraid, awed — because thirty-one chapters just told you that's the only thing about a human being worth praising when the beauty's gone.

Ashrei. Happy is the one. That's where the Psalms started. That's where Proverbs ends.

*Note on translation: 31:30 ishah yir'at-YHWH hi tit'hallal — "a woman that feareth the LORD, she shall be praised" (KJV) — rendered "a woman who stands in AWE of God — SHE is to be praised." This is the final yirah in the book and the closing bookend to 1:7 (reshit da'at) and 9:10 (t'chillat chochmah); the portrait it crowns (vv. 10–31) is incompatible with dread — she lo-tira (does not fear, v. 21, plain human yare) and tischaq l'yom acharon (laughs at the last day, v. 25). Hi is emphatic — "she, and not the charming or beautiful one." Sheqer ha-chen = charm is a lie; hevel ha-yofi = beauty is vapor (Eccl 1:2, Ps 39:5). 31:1 Lemuel melech massa — Massa as toponym (30:1); yiss'rattu immo = his mother trained him (musar verb). 31:2 bar (Aramaic) ×3; bar-n'darai = son of my vows (1 Sam 1:11). 31:8 p'tach-picha l'illem = open your mouth for the mute; b'nei chalof = children of passing/desolation — disputed; "those left desolate" follows the majority. 31:10–31 alphabetic acrostic, aleph→tav (cf. Ps 111, 112, 119; Lam 1–4). 31:10 eshet chayil — "virtuous woman" (KJV) — chayil = strength/valor/military might (Ruth 3:11; gibbor chayil 1 Sam 16:18); mi yimtza = who can find (= 20:6 ish emunim mi yimtza). 31:11 batach bah lev ba'lah = her husband's heart trusts in her (contrast 28:26). 31:13 b'chefetz kappeha = with the delight of her palms. 31:15 b'od laylah = while still night; choq l'na'aroteha = a ration for her maidservants (30:8 chuqqi; 27:27). 31:16 zam'mah sadeh = she considers/plans a field. 31:17 chag'rah v'oz motneha = girds her loins with strength. 31:18 lamp not extinguished (contrast 13:9, 20:20, 24:20). 31:20 kappah par'sah le-ani = spreads her palm to the poor (14:31, 19:17, 28:27). 31:21 lo-tira = does not fear — genuine human fear, absent. 31:22 shesh v'argaman = fine linen and purple — royal. 31:25 oz-v'hadar l'vushah = strength and majesty are her clothing — YHWH's own vesture (Ps 93:1, 104:1, 96:6); tischaq = laughs (8:30–31 m'sacheqet). 31:26 torat-chesed = teaching of loyal-love. 31:27 tzofiyah = keeps watch (15:3 tzofot); lechem atzlut = bread of sluggardness; the resemblance to Greek sophia is coincidental and not claimed. 31:28 va-y'ash'ruha = call her happy (ashrei, Ps 1:1, Prov 3:13). 31:29 asu chayil = have done valiantly. 31:31 t'nu-lah mi-p'ri yadeha = give her from the fruit of her hands; y'hal'luha va-sh'arim ma'aseha = let her works praise her in the gates. Liturgical use: sung at the Friday-night Sabbath table (Eshet Chayil). The Lady-Wisdom-embodied reading (1:20–33, 8, 9:1–6 → 31:10–31) is noted alongside the literal-wife reading. Yirah in this chapter: 31:30 — the last in the book.*
