# PROVERB 26 — Answer a Fool, Don't Answer a Fool

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Proverbs 26:1–28. Hezekiah's collection continued. This chapter is organized by character type — the fool (26:1–12), the sluggard (26:13–16), the meddler and gossip (26:17–22), the hater with smooth lips (26:23–28) — and contains the famous contradiction pair 26:4–5. Every verse present. No yirah verse. Companion: Prov 17:12; 22:13; 18:8; 2 Pet 2:22 (the dog returns).*

---

Chapter twenty-six is a rogues' gallery. Four portraits, each one a type you've met. The scribes lined them up so you'd recognize the face before the name.

Portrait one — the fool. Twelve verses, and the word kesil — the dull one — is in almost every one.

Verse one: **"Like snow in summer and rain at harvest, so honor isn't fitting for a fool."** It's not wrong, it's out of season. Snow in July isn't evil; it's ruinous. Honor a fool and you've rained on the harvest. Verse three: **"A whip for the horse, a bridle for the donkey, and a rod for the back of fools."** Shevet — the rod, again. Here it's animal control — whip, bridle, rod — listed with the horse and the donkey. That's not a parenting verse; that's the scribes saying the kesil responds to nothing subtler. It's descriptive of his level, not a command about children. Chapter thirteen's shepherd rod is a different tool in a different hand.

Verses four and five, side by side, on purpose:

**"Don't answer a fool according to his folly, or you'll be like him yourself."**

**"Answer a fool according to his folly, or he'll be wise in his own eyes."**

That's not a scribal error. Hezekiah's men put them next to each other. Proverbs are tools, not laws — and here's the book teaching you that in the plainest way it can: two true sayings that contradict, and you have to know which room you're in. Sometimes engaging the fool drags you to his level — walk away. Sometimes silence lets him think he won — answer. The wisdom isn't in either verse. It's in knowing which one it's time for. That's the whole book in two lines. Anybody who quotes one without the other has missed the point the editors were making. Verse two: **"Like a fluttering sparrow, a darting swallow, a curse without cause doesn't land."** Somebody cursed you for nothing? It won't land. It's a bird with no branch. Verse six: **"Whoever sends a message by a fool cuts off his own feet and drinks violence."** Verse seven: **"Like the legs hanging limp on a lame man is a proverb in the mouth of a fool."** He's got the saying — he just can't walk on it. Verse nine says it again: "Like a thorn branch in the hand of a drunk is a proverb in the mouth of a fool." He's waving the wisdom around and stabbing people with it. You've heard a fool quote Scripture. That's this.

Verse eight: **"Like tying a stone in a sling is giving honor to a fool."** You tied the stone in. It's not going anywhere. Chapter one's honor, wasted. Verse ten: **"Like an archer who wounds everyone is one who hires a fool or hires passersby."** The Hebrew's rough, but the picture holds: hire a fool and everybody gets hit.

Verse eleven: **"Like a dog returning to its vomit is a fool who repeats his folly."** Shoneh b'ivvalto — repeats his folly. Peter quotes this for the apostates in 2 Peter 2. It's the drunk's last line from chapter twenty-three — "I'll go find another" — as a dog. The fool doesn't fail once. He goes back.

And verse twelve, which turns the whole gallery on you: "Do you see a man wise in his own eyes? There's more hope for a fool than for him." After eleven verses on the kesil, the scribes say — there's someone worse. The one who thinks he's not in this chapter. Chapter three, verse seven; chapter twelve, verse fifteen. If you read the first eleven verses nodding at other people, verse twelve is for you.

Portrait two — the sluggard. Verses thirteen through sixteen, and the scribes are laughing.

"The sluggard says, 'There's a lion in the road! A lion in the streets!'" Chapter twenty-two's invented lion, now two lions. The excuse has grown. "As a door turns on its hinge, so the sluggard on his bed." He moves — just not forward. Back and forth, back and forth, same spot. "The sluggard buries his hand in the dish; he's too tired to bring it back to his mouth." Chapter nineteen's line, repeated — he got the hand in. "The sluggard is wiser in his own eyes than seven men who answer sensibly." Seven men. He's outvoted seven to one and he's still right. The sluggard isn't lazy about everything — he's industrious about excuses.

Portrait three — the meddler and the gossip. Verse seventeen: **"Like one who grabs a dog by the ears is a passerby who gets into a quarrel not his own."** You grabbed the dog. Now you can't let go, and you can't hold on. Every fight you inserted yourself into. Verses eighteen and nineteen: **"Like a madman shooting firebrands and arrows is a man who deceives his neighbor and says, 'I was only joking!'"** Ha-lo m'sacheq ani — "wasn't I just playing?" That's the word — sacheq — chapter eight's Wisdom playing before God at creation. Here it's the liar's cover story. "Just kidding" after the arrow's already in somebody.

Verses twenty and twenty-one: **"Without wood a fire goes out, and without a gossip a quarrel dies down. As charcoal to embers and wood to fire, so is a quarrelsome man for kindling strife."** Nirgan — the whisperer, chapter sixteen, eighteen. Every fight has a fuel source. Find the person adding wood. Often the fight doesn't need resolving — it needs starving. Verse twenty-two, chapter eighteen's line again word for word: "The words of a whisperer are like delicacies; they go down to the inner chambers of the belly." Gossip is tasty. That's why the fire never runs out of wood.

Portrait four — the hater with smooth lips. Verse twenty-three: **"Like silver glaze over earthenware are fervent lips with an evil heart."** Kesef sigim — silver dross, the skim off the furnace — painted on a clay pot. Looks like silver. Is a pot. Chapter five's honey lips, chapter seven's smooth talk. Verses twenty-four through twenty-six: **"The one who hates disguises it with his lips, but inside he stores up deceit. When he speaks graciously, don't believe him — for there are seven abominations in his heart. His hatred may be covered with deceit, but his wickedness will be exposed in the assembly."** Sheva to'evot — seven detestable things. Chapter six's seven. Same drawer. And tiggaleh ra'ato b'qahal — it comes out in public. Chapter ten — the twisted way will be known.

Verse twenty-seven: **"Whoever digs a pit falls into it; whoever rolls a stone, it rolls back on him."** The pit-digger. Psalm 7, Psalm 9, Psalm 35, Psalm 57, Proverbs 1, 5, 22. The scribes couldn't finish a chapter on the hater without it.

And verse twenty-eight, the last line: "A lying tongue hates those it crushes, and a flattering mouth works ruin."

L'shon-sheqer yisna dakkav. A lying tongue hates its victims. Read that — the liar doesn't just deceive you; he despises you for being deceived. And peh chalaq — a smooth mouth — ya'aseh midcheh — makes a stumbling, a downfall. Flattery isn't harmless. It's engineering. Someone laying a floor for you to fall through.

Here's the shape. Four portraits. The fool: honor him and it's snow in July; answer him and don't — know which room you're in; he goes back to his vomit — and the one who thinks he's not the fool is worse. The sluggard: two lions now, a door on a hinge, right against seven men. The meddler: grabbed the dog by the ears, "just kidding" after the arrow. The hater: silver dross on a clay pot, seven abominations, and he hates the people he fools.

Today: find the fire in your life that's still going, and stop looking for who started it. Look for who's adding wood. If it's you — put the log down and watch it go out.

*Note on translation: No yirah in this chapter. 26:3 shevet l'gev k'silim = a rod for the back of fools — listed with whip and bridle for animals; descriptive of the kesil's level, not a child-discipline text (cf. 10:13, 19:29; contrast 13:24 shepherd's rod). 26:4–5 deliberate juxtaposed contradiction — the editors' lesson that proverbs are situational tools, not universal laws. 26:2 Ketiv lo ("to him"), Qere lo ("not") — "does not land/come" (Qere followed). 26:7 dalyu shoqayim mi-pisseach = legs dangle from the lame. 26:8 k'tzror even b'margemah — "as he that bindeth a stone in a sling" — sense: pointless/self-defeating; alt. "like a bag of gems on a heap of stones." 26:10 MT very difficult (rav m'cholel-kol); rendered per majority "archer/master wounds all." 26:11 shoneh b'ivvalto = repeats his folly → 2 Pet 2:22. 26:12 the self-wise worse than the fool (3:7, 12:15, 28:11). 26:13 = 22:13 (lion doubled: shachal / ari). 26:15 ≈ 19:24. 26:16 mi-shiv'ah m'shivei ta'am = than seven who answer with sense. 26:17 machaziq b'oznei-kelev = grabs a dog by the ears. 26:19 ha-lo m'sacheq ani = "am I not playing?" — the sacheq root of 8:30–31, inverted. 26:20 nirgan = whisperer (16:28, 18:8). 26:22 = 18:8 verbatim. 26:23 kesef sigim = silver of dross; s'fatayim dol'qim — "burning lips" (KJV) — dolqim = fervent/ardent; some emend to chalaqim "smooth" (cf. v. 28). 26:25 sheva to'evot = seven abominations (6:16). 26:27 pit-digger (Ps 7:15, 9:15, 57:6; Prov 1:18, 5:22, 22:14). 26:28 l'shon-sheqer yisna dakkav = a lying tongue hates its crushed ones; peh chalaq ya'aseh midcheh = a smooth mouth makes a downfall.*
