# PROVERB 19 — Lend to God

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Proverbs 19:1–29. Tenth sayings chapter — 29 two-liners. Clustered by theme (every verse present): the poor and their friends, anger and the king, the mouth and the false witness, the sluggard, the household, and the one yirah verse (19:23 — awe as the pillow you sleep on). Companion: Prov 14:31, 17:5; Ps 41:1; Matt 25:40; Prov 3:24.*

---

Chapter nineteen is about the poor man — how the world treats him, how God treats him, and what the gap between those two tells you.

First handful — the poor and their friends. Verse one: **"Better a poor man who walks in integrity than one with twisted lips who is a fool."** Fourth "better a little." Verse four: **"Wealth adds many friends, but the poor is separated from his friend."** Me-re'ehu yippared — separated — chapter eighteen's word, the isolation word. Poverty does the separating. His one friend drifts. Verse seven: **"All the brothers of a poor man hate him — how much more do his friends go far from him! He pursues them with words, but they're gone."** M'radef amarim lo-hemmah — he chases them with speech, and they're not. The Hebrew of that last clause is broken — it just trails off. Like the poor man's sentence does.

Verse six: **"Many seek the favor of a generous man, and everyone is a friend to the one who gives gifts."** The rich man has a crowd; verse four's poor man has an empty room. Chapter fourteen said this. Chapter nineteen says it three times, because it's the most observable injustice there is.

And then verse seventeen, and this is the pivot of the chapter:

**"Whoever is kind to the poor lends to God, and He will repay him for his deed."**

Malveh YHWH — lends to God. Read that literally. When you give to a poor man, the receipt has God's signature on it. He's the borrower. You've just extended credit to heaven. And g'mulo y'shallem-lo — He'll pay back his deed. Not with interest, the book doesn't say that. Just: paid in full, by a debtor who's never defaulted. Chapter fourteen said oppressing the poor insults the Maker. Chapter nineteen says helping them makes the Maker your debtor. Jesus: "whatever you did to the least of these, you did to Me." Same ledger. Verse twenty-two: **"What a man desires is loyal-love, and better a poor man than a liar."** Ta'avat adam chasdo — a person's craving is chesed. What every human being actually wants, under everything, is loyal-love. Not wealth. To be kept.

Second handful — anger and the king. Verse three: **"A man's folly ruins his way, and his heart rages against God."** Yiz'af libbo — his heart storms at God. He wrecked his own road, then blamed the sky. Verse eleven: **"Good sense makes a man slow to anger, and it's his glory to overlook an offense."** He'erich apo — lengthens his nostril. Chapter fourteen, fifteen, sixteen — same idiom — but now with a payoff: tif'arto — his glory, his beauty, is passing over the offense. Not confronting every slight isn't weakness. It's ornament. Verse nineteen: **"A hot-tempered man pays the penalty; rescue him, and you'll have to do it again."** G'dol-chemah — great of heat. Bail him out, he'll be back. Anger is a repeat customer.

Verse twelve: **"A king's rage is like a lion's roar, but his favor is like dew on the grass."** Chapter sixteen's king as weather — dew this time, gentle, morning, life-giving. Verse ten: **"Luxury doesn't fit a fool; how much less for a slave to rule princes."** Verse six, again — the crowd around power. Verse twenty-five: **"Strike a mocker and the naive learn prudence; correct a discerning man and he gains knowledge."** Notice — the letz gets hit and someone else learns. The mocker himself doesn't. Chapter seventeen said one rebuke to a wise man beats a hundred blows to a fool. Here the blows to the fool are at best a demonstration for the people watching. And hochiach l'navon — correct the discerning — a word does it. The book is consistent: violence doesn't teach the target. Words teach the teachable.

Verse twenty-nine: **"Judgments are prepared for mockers, and beatings for the backs of fools."** Mahalumot — blows. Yes, the text says it. And read the previous verse's logic with it: this is what happens to the fool — consequence — not what the wise man is told to do. The fool's back will meet the stick because the fool walked into it. Chapter thirteen's shepherd's rod is a different thing.

Third handful — the mouth and the witness. Verse five: **"A false witness won't go unpunished, and whoever breathes lies won't escape."** Verse nine says it again almost word for word: "A false witness won't go unpunished, and whoever breathes lies will perish." Yafiach k'zavim — exhales lies, chapter twelve, fourteen. When the book repeats itself four verses apart, it's underlining. Verse twenty-eight: **"A worthless witness mocks at justice, and the mouth of the wicked swallows iniquity."** Y'valla-aven — gulps it. He doesn't just speak wrong — he drinks it. Verse twenty-seven: **"Stop listening to training, my son, and you'll stray from the words of knowledge."** The Hebrew's ironic — "go ahead, stop listening" — a father's sarcasm. Fourth handful — the sluggard. Verse fifteen: **"Laziness casts into a deep sleep, and the idle soul goes hungry."** Tardemah — the Genesis 2 sleep, the deep one Adam was in when his rib came out. Laziness puts you under. Verse twenty-four, and it's the book's funniest sluggard line: "The sluggard buries his hand in the dish and won't even bring it back to his mouth." He got the hand into the food. That's most of the work. And then — nope. Chapter twelve's man who caught the deer and didn't cook it. Same guy.

Fifth handful — the household. Verse thirteen: **"A foolish son is a disaster to his father, and a wife's quarreling is a constant dripping."** Delef tored — a dripping that doesn't stop. The leak you can't find. Verse fourteen, right behind it: "House and wealth are inherited from fathers, but a prudent wife is from God." Ishah maskalet — a wife with insight. You can inherit a house. You can't inherit her. She comes from God directly. Chapter eighteen: finds a wife, finds good. The book pairs the bad-marriage verse with the good-marriage verse on purpose — so you'd hear the difference is her wisdom, not her existence.

Verse eighteen: **"Train your son while there's hope, and don't set your heart on killing him."** Wait — read that. Yasser — train, musar's verb — while there is hope. And the second half: el-hamito al-tissa nafshecha — literally, "don't lift your soul to put him to death." Some translations soften it to "don't be a willing party to his death." Either way, the father has to say this — which means he's seen fathers whose discipline crossed the line into harm. This is the book's own guardrail on the rod verses. Train while there's hope — early, chapter thirteen — and never let the correction become the thing that destroys him. Proverbs knew what abusive fathers do, and it told them to stop.

Verse twenty-six: **"Whoever robs his father and drives out his mother is a shameful and disgraceful son."** Verse twenty: **"Listen to advice and accept training, so you may be wise in your later days."** B'acharitecha — in your afterward, Psalm 37's word. Verse two: **"Desire without knowledge isn't good, and whoever hurries with his feet misses the mark."** Chote — misses. Rushing is missing. Verse eight: **"Whoever gets heart loves his own soul; whoever keeps understanding finds good."** Chapter fifteen — ignoring training is self-contempt. Getting heart is self-love. The only kind the book endorses. Verse sixteen: **"Whoever keeps the command keeps his soul; whoever despises his ways will die."** Verse twenty-one: **"Many plans are in a man's heart, but the purpose of God will stand."** Atzat YHWH hi taqum — His counsel is what stands up. Chapter sixteen, verse nine.

And verse twenty-three, the yirah verse: "The AWE of God leads to life, and one rests satisfied, not visited by harm."

Yirat YHWH l'chayyim — awe toward life. Chapter ten: awe adds days. Chapter fourteen: awe is a fountain of life. Chapter nineteen: awe is the direction of life. And then — v'savea yalin — satisfied, he lodges overnight. Psalm 30's word — yalin, spends the night. The one with awe sleeps full. Bal-yippaqed ra — harm doesn't come visit. Now — put "fear" back in there. "The fear of the LORD leads to life, and one sleeps satisfied." Nobody sleeps on fear. Fear is what keeps you up. Awe is a pillow. Chapter three, verse twenty-four — "you'll lie down and your sleep will be sweet." That sweetness is this verse's awe. The English has you terrified into slumber. The Hebrew has you awed into rest.

Here's the shape. The world separates the poor man from his last friend; God signs the receipt when you give to him — you've lent to God. Overlooking an offense is your glory. A false witness, twice. The sluggard got his hand in the dish and quit. A prudent wife comes from God, not from your father's estate — and never let training become destruction. And awe is a pillow: you sleep satisfied and harm doesn't visit.

Today: find one poor person and lend to God. Then tonight, when you lie down, try the Hebrew instead of the English — not afraid, awed — and see which one you actually fall asleep on.
