# PROVERB 5 — Drink From Your Own Well

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Proverbs 5:1–23. The first full lecture on the "strange woman" — sexual faithfulness, framed as two wells: the stranger's honey that turns to wormwood, and your own cistern that God tells you to enjoy without shame. Companion: Prov 2:16–19; Song of Songs for the celebration side.*

---

Chapter two mentioned her in passing. Chapter five gives her the whole floor — and then, halfway through, turns and gives *your own marriage* the floor, in language most churches are too embarrassed to read out loud. Let's read both.

**"My son, pay attention to my wisdom; bend your ear to my understanding, so you can keep discretion and your lips can guard knowledge."** Same opening as always. Lean in. This is going to matter.

Verse three: **"Because the lips of the strange woman drip honey, and her mouth is smoother than oil."**

Notice the father doesn't lie to you. He doesn't say she's ugly or obviously bad. He says she's *sweet*. Honey. Smooth. Because if he told you temptation was ugly, you'd never recognize it when it showed up beautiful. Wisdom is honest about what the trap looks like from the front.

Verse four: **"But in the end she's bitter as wormwood, sharp as a double-edged sword. Her feet go down to death; her steps take hold of Sheol."**

Honey at the start, wormwood at the end. *La'anah* — the bitterest herb they had. And her road goes to the grave. Not hell — the grave. The silence. Chapter two said the same: her house sinks down to the shades. The affair isn't punished by fire. It's punished by *death* — of the marriage, of the trust, of the self you used to be. Something dies that doesn't come back.

Verse six: **"She doesn't think about the path of life; her tracks wander and she doesn't even know it."** That's the key to the whole type. She's not a villain plotting. She's *lost*, and she's inviting you to be lost with her. She doesn't know where she's going. Why would you follow?

Verses seven through fourteen, the consequences. **"So now, sons, listen to me; don't turn from the words of my mouth. Keep your way far from her; don't go near the door of her house —"**

Chapter four said "go around" the wicked road. Same word. Don't walk past the door. Don't test yourself. **"— or you'll give your honor to others and your years to someone cruel."** Verse ten: **"strangers will feast on your strength, and your labor will end up in a foreigner's house."**

That's not a metaphor. That's the divorce settlement. That's child support to a house you don't live in. That's the years of work that end up furnishing someone else's life. The father is describing the economic wreckage of adultery three thousand years before the lawyers got involved.

And then verses eleven through fourteen — the groan at the end. **"You'll groan at your end, when your flesh and body are consumed, and you'll say: How I hated training! How my heart despised correction! I didn't listen to my teachers. I didn't bend my ear. And now I'm almost completely ruined, in front of the whole assembly."**

*Musar* and *tochachat*. Training and correction. The man at the end is not saying "I wish I'd been scared of God." He's saying "I wish I'd let myself be *coached*." That's the regret. Public ruin — *b'toch qahal v'edah* — in front of everyone — because he wouldn't take the reps when they were offered for free.

Then verse fifteen, and the whole chapter turns. Watch what the father does.

**"Drink water from your own cistern, running water from your own well."**

He's talking about your wife. And he's not whispering. Verses sixteen and seventeen: **"Should your springs be scattered outside, streams of water in the streets? Let them be yours alone, not for strangers with you."** Your intimacy is a *spring* — that's the word from 4:23, the wellspring. Don't pour it in the street. Keep it for the one it's meant for.

And then verse eighteen and nineteen, and I want you to hear this because Proverbs is not a prudish book: **"Let your fountain be blessed, and rejoice in the wife of your youth — a loving doe, a graceful deer. Let her breasts satisfy you at all times; be intoxicated with her love always."**

*Tishgeh* — that's the word. Be *drunk*. Stagger. Lose yourself. God's wisdom book says: be intoxicated with your own wife, always. The Bible is not against passion. It's against passion *poured in the street*. It wants your spring to be a fire hose *at home*. Song of Songs is eight chapters of this and it's in the same canon.

So the contrast isn't sex versus no sex. It's stolen honey that turns to wormwood versus your own well that stays sweet. The father is saying: you're not being asked to give something up. You're being asked to *drink deep* from the right well and stop being thirsty at the wrong one.

Verse twenty: **"Why would you be intoxicated with a stranger, and embrace the bosom of a foreign woman?"** Same word — *tishgeh*. He's saying, you can be drunk on one or the other. Choose the one that doesn't end in wormwood.

Verse twenty-one: **"Because the eyes of God are on a man's ways, and He weighs all his paths."** Not surveillance. *Mefales* — He levels, He measures. He sees the whole path, including the end you can't see from the door. That's why He tells you not to go in. Not because He's angry — because He can see the wormwood from here.

Verses twenty-two and twenty-three, the close: **"His own wrongs will trap the wicked man; he'll be held by the ropes of his own missing-the-mark. He'll die for lack of training; in the greatness of his folly he'll go astray."**

Ropes of his own making. Pit-digger. You know this move by now — Psalm 7, Psalm 9, Proverbs 1. And the cause of death, verse twenty-three: lack of *musar*. Not lack of fear. Not lack of rules. Lack of *coaching received*. He was offered the reps and said no.

Here's the shape. The stranger's lips drip honey, and the father admits it — but honey turns to wormwood and her feet go to the grave. Stay away from the door. Your strength and your years will end up in someone else's house. And then the turn: don't scatter your spring in the street — drink from your own well, be *intoxicated* with the wife of your youth, always. God isn't against the fire. He's against the arson.

Today: if you're married, go be drunk on the right well tonight — actually delight in the one you promised. If you're near a door you shouldn't be near, walk the other way *before* you can smell the honey.

*Note on translation: 5:3* ishah zarah *— "strange woman" — is a type (the covenant-breaker; cf. 2:16–17), not a gender indictment; the father-to-son frame produces a female tempter. 5:4* la'anah *= wormwood, bitterest herb. 5:5* Sheol *— "hell" in KJV — rendered "the grave"; consistent with the Psalms treatment; 2:18* refa'im *parallel. 5:6 the subject of* pen-tefales *is ambiguous (she doesn't consider / lest you consider); rendered as her not watching the path, following the majority reading. 5:12, 5:23* musar *= training; 5:12* tochachat *= correction — the regret is over refused coaching, not over insufficient fear. 5:14* b'toch qahal v'edah *= in the midst of assembly and congregation — public shame. 5:15–18* bor */* be'er */* maqor *= cistern / well / fountain — sexual metaphors for the wife, drawing on 4:23's* totz'ot *(springs). 5:19* dadeha *= her breasts (some translations soften to "love");* tishgeh *= stagger/be intoxicated — the same verb reused deliberately at 5:20 for the stranger, making the contrast one of* which *intoxication, not intoxication vs. sobriety. 5:21* mefales *= weighs/levels. 5:22* chavlei chattato *= ropes of his own missing-the-mark. No yirah in this chapter.*
