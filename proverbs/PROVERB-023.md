# PROVERB 23 — Wings Like an Eagle

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Proverbs 23:1–35. Thirty Sayings continued (sayings 6–19): the ruler's table, riches that fly off, the stingy host, the boundary stone, the third rod verse (23:13–14), the father's plea to a son (23:15–28), and the long drunkard's portrait (23:29–35). Yirah at 23:17. Companion: Amenemope chs. 7, 11, 23; Prov 20:1; Ps 73 (envy of the wicked).*

---

Chapter twenty-three is the Thirty Sayings at full length — the two-liners give way to little paragraphs, and the teacher sits down across from you. It's the most intimate chapter since the father's lectures in one through nine, and it ends with the most vivid drunk in the Bible.

Saying six — the ruler's table. Verses one through three: **"When you sit down to eat with a ruler, consider carefully what's in front of you. Put a knife to your throat if you're a man of big appetite. Don't crave his delicacies — it's deceptive food."**

Lechem k'zavim — bread of lies. The powerful man's table has a price on it, and the price isn't on the menu. Amenemope told Egyptian civil servants the exact same thing: don't eat greedily in front of the boss — it's a test. The knife-to-the-throat is comic. Restrain yourself that hard. Every free meal from a man who wants something is a hook with food on it.

Saying seven — riches with wings. Verses four and five: **"Don't wear yourself out to get rich; have the sense to stop. Will you set your eyes on it? It's gone — because it makes itself wings and flies to the heavens like an eagle."**

Ta'uf ha-shamayim k'nesher — it flies off like an eagle. Amenemope: "riches make themselves wings like geese and fly to the sky." Israel swapped the goose for an eagle and kept the picture. You look at the money and it isn't there. Chapter thirteen's vapor. Chapter eleven's inherited wind. And the phrase in the middle — mi-binatcha chadal — literally "from your understanding, cease." Be smart enough to stop. Ambition without a brake is not wisdom. Knowing when enough is enough is.

Saying eight — the stingy host. Verses six through eight: **"Don't eat the bread of a man with an evil eye; don't crave his delicacies. He's the kind who's always calculating — 'eat and drink,' he says to you, but his heart isn't with you. You'll vomit up the morsel you ate and waste your pleasant words."** Ra-ayin — evil of eye. Chapter twenty-two's good eye, flipped. The stingy man feeds you and resents it, and you can taste the resentment. The Hebrew for *his* calculating is ki-k'mo-sha'ar b'nafsho — "like a gate-keeper in his soul," counting every bite through the gate. You'll throw the meal up. And your thank you was wasted on him. Don't eat where you're not wanted, no matter how good the food.

Sayings nine through eleven — fools, fences, and the child. Verse nine: **"Don't speak in a fool's ears — he'll despise the sense in your words."** Chapter nine — you can't reach the letz. Save your breath. Verses ten and eleven: **"Don't move the ancient boundary stone or encroach on the fields of orphans, for their Redeemer is strong; He'll plead their case against you."** Go'alam chazaq — their Redeemer, the kinsman-redeemer word, Psalm 19's goel, Ruth's Boaz. The orphan has no family to fight for his fence. So God is the family. Chapter twenty-two, verse twenty-three. Move a fatherless kid's fence and you've picked a fight with his big brother, and his big brother is God.

Verse twelve: **"Apply your heart to training and your ears to words of knowledge."** Musar — coaching. It leads straight into the hardest lines.

Verses thirteen and fourteen: **"Don't withhold training from a child; if you strike him with the rod, he won't die. Strike him with the rod and you'll rescue his soul from Sheol."**

Third rod verse, and this one does say strike — takkennu. I won't pretend it doesn't. So let's read it honestly, all of it.

First, the frame: al-timna mi-na'ar musar — don't withhold coaching from the kid. That's the command. The rest is the ancient teacher's assumption of how coaching happens in his world. Second, lo yamut — "he won't die." Why would the teacher have to say that? Because he's heard fathers use it as an excuse to do nothing — "I don't want to hurt him" — and he's answering: correcting your child won't kill him. It's permission to parent. Third, the goal: mi-sh'ol tatzil — rescue him from the grave. Not hell — the grave. The premature death that comes from unrestrained folly; chapter seven's ox. The rod is between the kid and the cliff. That's Psalm 23's rod, hooking the sheep back.

And now the guardrails the book itself put around this verse. Chapter nineteen, verse eighteen: train while there's hope, and never let it become the thing that destroys him — the Hebrew explicitly forbids that. Chapter seventeen, verse ten: one rebuke does more in a wise heart than a hundred blows in a fool's. Chapter nineteen, verse twenty-five: the blow teaches the bystander, not the one hit. Chapter twenty-two, verse eight: the man who rules by the rod of his fury runs out of rod. The book gave you the shepherd's rod in thirteen and then spent six chapters telling you what it isn't for. Read all of it, or don't quote any of it. The teacher's culture had corporal discipline; the teacher's principle is: don't abandon the kid to his own folly, because that road ends in the grave. You are allowed — you are required — to find the version of that principle that works without the belt. God found one for you. Chapter three, verse twelve. Go be that father.

Sayings twelve through sixteen — the father's plea. This is where the teacher's voice cracks. Verses fifteen and sixteen: **"My son, if your heart is wise, my heart will be glad too — yes, mine. My innermost being will rejoice when your lips speak what's right."** Gam ani — even I, me too. He's not teaching. He's hoping. Verse nineteen: **"Hear, my son, and be wise, and direct your heart in the way."** Verse twenty-two: **"Listen to your father who gave you life, and don't despise your mother when she's old."** Both parents again. Verses twenty-four and twenty-five: **"The father of a right-standing child will greatly rejoice; whoever fathers a wise son delights in him. Let your father and mother be glad; let her who bore you rejoice."** Verse twenty-six: **"My son, give me your heart, and let your eyes delight in my ways."** T'nah-libbecha li — give me your heart. Not your obedience. Your heart. That's the whole ask, and it's the same one God makes.

And in the middle of the plea, verses seventeen and eighteen, the awe verse:

**"Don't let your heart envy mark-missers, but be in the AWE of God all day long — because surely there is a future, and your hope won't be cut off."**

Al-y'qanne libbecha ba-chatta'im — don't let your heart burn with envy at the ones who get away with it. That's Psalm 73 — "I envied the arrogant when I saw the prosperity of the wicked." Every honest person has stood there. And the answer isn't "be afraid of God." The Hebrew is *ki* im-b'yirat YHWH kol-ha-yom — literally, "but [be] in awe of God all the day." Stay in the awe. All day. The antidote to envying the cheater is standing in the canyon long enough that his sports car looks small. And then the reason: ki im-yesh acharit — surely there's an afterward, Psalm 37's word. Tiqvat'cha lo tikkaret — your hope won't be cut off. Fear doesn't give you a future. Awe does — because it puts you next to the One who owns the afterward.

Sayings seventeen and eighteen — wine and women, briefly. Verses twenty and twenty-one: **"Don't be among winebibbers or gluttonous eaters of meat — the drunkard and the glutton come to poverty, and drowsiness clothes a man in rags."** Verses twenty-seven and twenty-eight: **"A prostitute is a deep pit; a strange woman is a narrow well. She lies in wait like a robber and increases the treacherous among men."** Chapter twenty-two's pit, chapter seven's ambush. Nothing new — the teacher's repeating the father because the son still hasn't heard it.

Saying nineteen — the drunk. Verses twenty-nine through thirty-five. This is the one.

"Who has woe? Who has sorrow? Who has quarrels? Who has complaining? Who has wounds for no reason? Who has bloodshot eyes?" Six questions, one answer: "Those who linger long over wine, who go looking for mixed drinks."

Then the warning, and it's beautiful, which is the point: "Don't look at wine when it's red, when it sparkles in the cup, when it goes down smoothly." Yit'hallech b'meisharim — it walks in straight — it goes down easy. The teacher doesn't say wine is ugly. He says it's gorgeous. Chapter five's honey. Chapter seven's Egyptian linen. The trap is always pretty from the front.

"In the end it bites like a snake and stings like a viper." The morning-after. "Your eyes will see strange things, and your heart will speak twisted things." Zarot — strange things — the stranger word. Hallucinations, and the mouth saying things you don't mean. "You'll be like one lying down in the middle of the sea, like one lying on top of a mast." The spins. The room moving. The teacher has been there or watched someone there — nobody writes "lying on top of a mast" from a textbook.

And then verse thirty-five, the last line, in the drunk's own voice: "'They hit me — I didn't feel it. They beat me — I didn't know it. When will I wake up? I'll go find another drink.'"

Osif avaqshennu od. I'll seek it again. That's the disease in one sentence. He got beaten and didn't feel it. He knows he's been beaten. And the very next thought is more. Anybody who's been in a room with recovery knows that voice exactly. Proverbs wrote it down three thousand years ago and didn't blink. The teacher doesn't moralize. He just lets the drunk finish his sentence, and the sentence is the whole argument.

Here's the shape. The powerful man's table is a hook. Riches grow wings — be smart enough to stop. Don't eat where the host resents you. God is the orphan's big brother. Don't abandon the kid to his own folly — and read the book's own guardrails on how. Envy the cheater? Stand in awe all day instead; awe has a future and fear doesn't. Give your father your heart. And the drunk's last line is "I'll go find another." Don't let it be yours.

Today: if you're chasing money, find your brake — from your understanding, cease. If you're envying someone who got away with it, spend the day in the canyon instead. And if verse thirty-five is your voice, or someone's you love — the teacher heard it too. Wake up is still on the table.

*Note on translation: 23:17 ki im-b'yirat YHWH kol-ha-yom — "but be thou in the fear of the LORD all the day long" (KJV) — rendered "be in the AWE of God all day long"; the context is envy of the wicked (Ps 73) and the promise of acharit (a future, 37:37) — awe, not dread, is the posture that has a future. 23:1–3 ≈ Amenemope ch. 23; lechem k'zavim = bread of lies. 23:4–5 ≈ Amenemope ch. 7 (geese → eagle); mi-binatcha chadal = from your understanding, cease; Ketiv/Qere on ta'uf/ya'uf. 23:6 ra-ayin = evil of eye (contrast 22:9). 23:7 ki-k'mo-sha'ar b'nafsho — very difficult; "like a gatekeeper in his soul / as he reckons within himself"; KJV "as he thinketh in his heart, so is he" is a famous misreading — the verse is about the host's calculating, not a general maxim about self-image. 23:10–11 ≈ Amenemope ch. 6; go'alam chazaq = their Redeemer is strong (kinsman-redeemer, Ps 19:14). 23:13–14 takkennu va-shevet = strike him with the rod — the only rod verse with an explicit striking verb; lo yamut = he will not die (permission-to-parent against neglect); mi-sh'ol tatzil — "deliver his soul from hell" (KJV) — from the GRAVE, premature death by folly (cf. 7:27); shevet = shepherd's rod (13:24 note); guardrails 17:10, 19:18, 19:25, 22:8, 3:12. 23:15–16 gam ani = even I. 23:26 t'nah-libbecha li = give me your heart. 23:27 shuchah amuqqah / be'er tzarah = deep pit / narrow well. 23:29–35 the drunkard portrait; yit'hallech b'meisharim = goes down smoothly/straight; zarot = strange things (the zar root of the strange woman); b'rosh chibbel = on top of a mast; 23:35 osif avaqshennu od = I will seek it yet again.
