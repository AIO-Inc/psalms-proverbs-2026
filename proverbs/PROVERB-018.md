# PROVERB 18 — The Name Is a Tower

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Proverbs 18:1–24. Ninth sayings chapter — 24 two-liners. Clustered by theme (every verse present): isolation and pride, the mouth (deep waters, death and life in the tongue), the strong tower, the poor and the rich, hearing both sides, and the closing pair on friendship and marriage. No yirah verse. Companion: Prov 17:17; Ps 61:3 (tower); Ps 18:2; Prov 15:33.*

---

Chapter eighteen opens with a man who's walked away from everybody.

**"Whoever isolates himself seeks his own desire; he breaks out against all sound judgment."**

Nifrad — the one who has separated himself. Chapter sixteen and seventeen used the same root for the whisperer who separates friends. Here the man's done it to himself. And notice the diagnosis: he's not seeking solitude — he's seeking ta'avah, his own craving. The man who pulls away from everyone isn't usually pursuing peace. He's pursuing himself, with no one around to say no. And yitgalla — he breaks out, picks a fight — against anything sensible. Loneliness makes you combative. You've seen it.

First handful — fools and pride. Verse two: **"A fool takes no pleasure in understanding, but only in airing his own heart."** B'hitgallot libbo — in uncovering his heart. He doesn't want to learn. He wants to be heard. Verse three: **"When wickedness comes, contempt comes too, and with dishonor, reproach."** They arrive as a set. Verse twelve: **"Before destruction a man's heart is proud, but before honor is humility."** Lifnei shever — before the break. Chapter sixteen, verse eighteen, and chapter fifteen, verse thirty-three, fused into one line. Pride is the precursor to the break; humility is the precursor to the honor. Two roads, two front-runners. Whichever one's out front, you know what's coming behind it.

Verse nine: **"Whoever is slack in his work is brother to the destroyer."** Ach hu l'va'al mashchit — he's kin to the wrecker. The sluggard and the vandal end up with the same result; one just took longer.

Second handful — the mouth. Verse four: **"The words of a man's mouth are deep waters; the fountain of wisdom is a flowing brook."** Mayim amuqqim — deep waters. A person's speech goes all the way down; you're never hearing the bottom. Verse six: **"A fool's lips bring strife, and his mouth invites a beating."** Verse seven: **"A fool's mouth is his ruin, and his lips are a snare to his soul."** Moqesh nafsho — the trap for his own life. He set it himself, with his teeth. Verse eight: **"The words of a whisperer are like delicacies; they go down to the inner chambers of the belly."** K'mit'lahamim — like treats. Gossip is tasty. That's why it works. It goes down easy and sits in your gut. This exact line repeats at 26:22.

Verse thirteen: **"Whoever answers before he hears — it's folly to him and shame."** Meshiv davar b'terem yishma — returns a word before hearing. You know that person. You've been that person. The answer was loaded before the question finished. Verse seventeen: **"The first to plead his case seems right — until the other comes and examines him."** Rishon b'rivo tzaddiq — the first one sounds righteous. Always. Every story sounds airtight until the other side walks in. Never rule on one side.

Verse twenty: **"From the fruit of his mouth a man's stomach is filled; the produce of his lips satisfies him."** You eat your own words — chapter twelve, again. And then verse twenty-one, the line the whole cluster stands on:

**"Death and life are in the hand of the tongue, and those who love it eat its fruit."**

Mavet v'chayyim b'yad-lashon. Death and life — death first. Not "the tongue can be positive or negative." Death and life — the two ultimate things — are in the hand of the tongue, like it's a person with a grip. Chapter fifteen said the twisted tongue breaks the spirit; chapter twelve said it stabs like a sword. Here it's the whole range: your tongue can kill and it can resurrect, and you eat the fruit either way. James read this — "the tongue is a fire… it can bless and curse from the same mouth." He got it here.

Third handful — the tower. Verse ten:

**"The name of God is a strong tower; the right-standing runs into it and is set high."**

Migdal-oz — a tower of strength. Psalm 61 — "You have been a strong tower against the enemy." Psalm 18 — my rock, my fortress. In a walled city, the tower was the last place — when the wall fell, you ran there. And the good man runs — yarutz — he doesn't stroll. This is the emergency exit. And nisgav — he's lifted, set high, out of reach. The name — shem — is the personal name YHWH, the one the old Bibles hid behind "LORD" and this series renders simply as God. The name itself is the tower. Not a place. A who.

Verse eleven, immediately after, the counterfeit: "A rich man's wealth is his strong city, and like a high wall in his own imagination." B'maskito — in his imagination. Same words — strong, high — but the tower in verse ten is real and the wall in verse eleven is a picture in his head. The rich man thinks his money is a wall. Verse ten's man knows his God is a tower. One of them's going to find out.

Fourth handful — poor and rich, and the gift. Verse twenty-three: **"The poor man speaks in pleas, but the rich answers roughly."** Tachanunim — supplications, begging. The poor ask; the rich bark. Observation, not approval. Verse sixteen: **"A man's gift opens the way for him and brings him before the great."** That's just how doors work — a gift gets you in the room. Verse five: **"It's not good to show partiality to the wicked, to push aside the right-standing in judgment."** S'et p'nei rasha — lifting the face of the wicked. Giving him the nod because of who he is. Verse eighteen: **"Casting the lot ends disputes and separates the powerful."** Chapter sixteen's lot, again — when two strong men can't settle it, throw the dice and let God decide. It ends it, yashbit — Sabbath verb. The argument gets to rest.

Verse fourteen: **"A man's spirit sustains him in sickness, but a crushed spirit — who can bear it?"** Ruach n'khe'ah — chapter fifteen, seventeen. The spirit can carry a sick body. Nothing carries a crushed spirit. That's why the book spends so much time protecting it. Verse fifteen: **"The heart of the discerning acquires knowledge, and the ear of the wise seeks it."** Yiqneh — buys it, chapter four's "get wisdom." Verse nineteen: **"A brother offended is harder than a strong city, and quarrels are like the bars of a fortress."** Ach nifsha — a brother who's been wronged. The same tower-and-wall language, but now the wall is between you. Family rifts are the hardest fortresses to take. The book knows.

And the closing pair. Verse twenty-two: **"Whoever finds a wife finds good, and obtains favor from God."** Matza ishah matza tov — finds a wife, finds good. The Hebrew just says tov — the Genesis word, "and it was good." A wife is tov. Not "a good wife" — the old Bibles add "good" to the wife and the Hebrew doesn't; it puts the good in what you found. She's the good. Chapter five's own well, chapter twelve's crown, chapter thirty-one's woman of strength — and ratzon, favor, God's delight word. Finding her is God smiling.

And verse twenty-four, the last line, the one that answers chapter seventeen:

**"A man of many companions comes to ruin, but there is a friend who sticks closer than a brother."**

Ish re'im l'hitro'ea — a man of friends, to be broken. The Hebrew puns — re'im (friends) and hitro'ea (shattered) sound alike. Many acquaintances, no anchor. And then: ohev daveq me'ach — a lover who clings closer than a brother. Daveq — the Genesis 2 word, a man cleaves to his wife. There is a friend who cleaves. Chapter seventeen said a brother is born for adversity. Chapter eighteen says there's one who beats even the brother. Many companions is a crowd. One who cleaves is a tower.

Here's the shape. The isolated man is chasing himself with no one to say no. A fool wants to be heard, not to learn. Death and life are in the hand of the tongue — and you eat the fruit. The name is a strong tower you run into; the rich man's wall is a picture in his head. Hear both sides before you rule. A wife is good — the Genesis word. And there's a friend who cleaves closer than a brother.

Today: check whether you've been the nifrad — the one who pulled away and picked fights. If so, run into the tower first, then call the one who cleaves. And before you answer anyone today, let them finish.

*Note on translation: No yirah in this chapter. 18:1 nifrad = one who separates himself (root of mafrid, 16:28, 17:9); ta'avah = craving; yitgalla = breaks out/quarrels. 18:2 b'hitgallot libbo = in uncovering his heart. 18:4 mayim amuqqim = deep waters. 18:7 moqesh nafsho = snare of his soul. 18:8 k'mit'lahamim = like dainties — hapax, sense uncertain but "delicacies" is the majority; = 26:22 verbatim. 18:9 ach l'va'al mashchit = brother to a master of destruction. 18:10 migdal-oz shem YHWH = the name of God is a strong tower (Ps 61:3, 18:2); yarutz = runs; nisgav = is set high/inaccessible. 18:11 b'maskito = in his imagination/imagery — the wall is mental; contrast with v. 10's real tower. 18:12 fuses 16:18 and 15:33. 18:13 meshiv davar b'terem yishma = returns a word before he hears. 18:14 ruach n'khe'ah = crushed spirit (15:13, 17:22). 18:17 rishon b'rivo tzaddiq = the first in his case is righteous [seeming]. 18:18 yashbit = causes to cease (Sabbath root). 18:19 ach nifsha — text uncertain; MT "a brother transgressed against"; LXX differs — MT followed. 18:21 mavet v'chayyim b'yad-lashon = death and life in the hand of the tongue; cf. James 3:5–10. 18:22 matza ishah matza tov — KJV/LXX add "good" to "wife"; MT has no adjective: the wife is the tov; ratzon = favor/delight. 18:23 tachanunim = supplications. 18:24 ish re'im l'hitro'ea — Ketiv/Qere and root disputed (ra'ah friend vs. ra'a' shatter); the pun is likely intentional; ohev daveq = a loving one who cleaves (Gen 2:24 davaq).*
