# PROVERB 28 — Bold as a Lion

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Proverbs 28:1–28. Hezekiah's collection continued — 28 sayings, with a strong political edge: rulers, the poor, the law (torah), and the confession verse (28:13). Clustered by theme (every verse present): the flight of the guilty and the boldness of the good, rulers and the poor, torah-keepers, the confession, the greedy and the hasty, and the man who trusts his own heart. No yirah verse (28:14's "feareth alway" is pachad — a genuine dread word, and it's rendered as such). Companion: Ps 32:3–5; Ps 51; 1 John 1:9; Prov 21:1; Ps 1.*

---

Chapter twenty-eight opens with a man running from nobody.

**"The wicked flee when no one pursues, but the right-standing are bold as a lion."**

Nasu v'ein-rodef rasha. He ran, and there's no pursuer. That's the guilty man's whole life — the footsteps behind him are his own. And the right-standing man — kikfir yivtach — is confident like a young lion. Not aggressive. Unhurried. He's not looking over his shoulder because there's nothing back there. Chapter fourteen — awe is a fortress. Chapter nineteen — awe is a pillow. Here's what it looks like from outside: the lion doesn't check the exits.

First handful — rulers and the poor. This chapter thinks about government more than any since sixteen. Verse two: **"When a land rebels it has many rulers, but with a man of understanding and knowledge it endures."** Coups breed coups. Stability comes from one wise head. Verse three: **"A poor man who oppresses the poor is a driving rain that leaves no food."** Gever rash v'osheq dallim — a poor man crushing the poor. That's the worst version — the one who got a little power and used it on people below him. Rain that destroys the crop instead of feeding it. Verse fifteen: **"Like a roaring lion or a charging bear is a wicked ruler over a helpless people."** Chapter seventeen's bereaved bear, now on the throne. Verse sixteen: **"A ruler who lacks understanding is a great oppressor, but the one who hates unjust gain will prolong his days."**

Verse twelve: **"When the right-standing triumph there's great glory, but when the wicked rise, people hide."** Y'chuppas adam — a person gets searched for — everyone's gone to ground. Verse twenty-eight, the last line, says it again: "When the wicked rise, people hide; when they perish, the right-standing increase." The scribes bookend the chapter with it. You can read a regime by whether the street is full or empty. Chapter eleven — when the right-standing prosper, the city rejoices.

Verse eight: **"Whoever increases his wealth by interest and profit gathers it for one who is kind to the poor."** B'neshech v'tarbit — by biting and increase — the loan-shark words. The usurer is saving up for the generous man; it'll end up in his hands. Chapter thirteen, verse twenty-two. Verse eleven: **"A rich man is wise in his own eyes, but a poor man with understanding sees through him."** Yachq'rennu — searches him out. The poor man with sense can read the rich fool like a book. Verse six: **"Better a poor man who walks in integrity than a rich man whose ways are crooked."** Chapter nineteen, verse one — repeated. Verse twenty-seven: **"Whoever gives to the poor will not lack, but whoever hides his eyes will get many curses."** Ma'lim einav — hides his eyes. The verse doesn't say refuses. It says doesn't look. That's how most people fail the poor — not with a "no," with an averted gaze. Chapter twenty-one — shut your ear and yours gets shut. Here it's the eyes.

Second handful — torah-keepers. The word torah — teaching, instruction, not "law" — shows up four times. Verse four: **"Those who forsake the teaching praise the wicked, but those who keep it fight them."** Yitgaru vam — they contend with them. Keeping the teaching isn't passive. Verse seven: **"A discerning son keeps the teaching, but a companion of gluttons shames his father."** Verse nine: **"Whoever turns his ear from hearing the teaching — even his prayer is detestable."** To'evah — the idolatry drawer, chapter eleven — applied to prayer. Stop your ears to the instruction, and even your praying gets filed with the idols. Chapter fifteen — the sacrifice of the wicked is detestable. Verse five: **"Evil men don't understand justice, but those who seek God understand it all."** Verse eighteen: **"Whoever walks in integrity will be saved, but the one who is crooked in his ways will fall at once."** Verse ten: **"Whoever leads the upright astray on an evil path will fall into his own pit, but the blameless inherit good."** Pit-digger. The scribes never tire of it.

Third handful — the confession. Verse thirteen:

**"Whoever conceals his wrongs won't prosper, but whoever confesses and forsakes them finds compassion."**

M'chasseh f'sha'av — the one who covers his transgressions. Chapter ten said love covers — same verb — but that's covering someone else's wrong. Covering your own is the opposite move. Psalm 32 — "when I kept silent my bones wasted away… then I confessed, and You forgave." David knew exactly this verse's shape. Modeh v'ozev — confesses and abandons. Both. You can confess without abandoning — that's a hobby. You can abandon without confessing — that's pretending it didn't happen. The one who does both — y'rucham — is shown compassion. Racham, the womb-word, God's Exodus 34 word. First John — "if we confess, He is faithful to forgive." Every step-five in every church basement is this verse.

And verse fourteen, right behind it, and read it carefully because the English has damaged it:

**"Happy is the man who is always in dread, but whoever hardens his heart falls into calamity."**

Your Bible says "happy is the man that feareth alway," and you've heard it preached as "fear God constantly." But the word here is not yirah. It's m'fached — pachad — the genuine fear word, the panic word, chapter one's "dread of harm," chapter three's "sudden fear." So what's the verse saying? Look at what it's paired with — verse thirteen's confession, and the hardened heart in the second line. This is the awareness of your own capacity to fall. The man who's "always in dread" is the man who knows he could go back to the vomit, chapter twenty-six, and stays alert. Ashrei — happy — is the person who never stops watching himself. The contrast is maqsheh libbo — the one who hardens his heart, Pharaoh's word, and drops his guard. So: this is not awe of God — that's yirah, and it's not here. It's healthy dread of your own weakness. Anyone in recovery knows the difference. Complacency kills. The scribes said: happy is the one who stays scared of the slip. And the next verse's roaring lion is what happens when nobody's watching the king's heart either.

Fourth handful — the greedy and the hasty. Verse nineteen: **"Whoever works his land will have plenty of bread, but whoever chases fantasies will have plenty of poverty."** Sova-rish — he'll be full of poverty. Chapter twelve, verse eleven. Verse twenty: **"A faithful man abounds with blessings, but whoever hurries to get rich won't go unpunished."** Verse twenty-two: **"A man with an evil eye hurries after wealth and doesn't know that want will come on him."** Ra-ayin — the stingy eye, chapter twenty-three. Verse twenty-five: **"A greedy man stirs up strife, but whoever trusts in God will prosper."** R'chav-nefesh — wide of soul, a soul with a big mouth. Verse twenty-one: **"Showing partiality isn't good — a man will do wrong for a piece of bread."** Verse twenty-four: **"Whoever robs his father or mother and says, 'It's not wrong' — he's a companion of a destroyer."** Ein-pasha — "no transgression." He's told himself it's fine. Chapter twenty-six's "just kidding." Verse seventeen: **"A man burdened with the guilt of blood will flee to the pit; let no one support him."** Ad-bor yanus — he flees to the pit — the grave, not hell. The runner from verse one, arrived.

Verse twenty-three: **"Whoever rebukes a man will afterward find more favor than one who flatters with the tongue."** Acharai — afterward. Not right away. The rebuke stings, and then — later — they thank you. Chapter twenty-seven's faithful wound. Flattery gets the smile now and the contempt later.

And verse twenty-six, the one to leave with:

**"Whoever trusts in his own heart is a fool, but whoever walks in wisdom will be delivered."**

Boteach b'libbo hu k'sil. Trusting your own heart makes you a kesil. The culture says the opposite — "follow your heart." Proverbs, which has spent twenty-eight chapters telling you to guard your heart, chapter four, says: guard it, yes — trust it, no. The heart is the wellspring; it's also deceitful above all things, Jeremiah 17. Verse thirteen said confess; verse fourteen said stay alert; verse twenty-six says don't hand the wheel to the thing you're supposed to be watching. Walk in wisdom — the outside thing, God's thing — and yimmalet, you get out.

Here's the shape. The wicked run from nobody; the right-standing don't check the exits. A poor man crushing the poor is rain that kills the crop. When the wicked rise, the street empties. Stop your ears to the teaching and even your prayer is filed with the idols. Confess and abandon — both — and find the womb-word. Stay in healthy dread of your own slip; the hardened heart falls. And don't trust your own heart — guard it, and walk in something wiser.

Today: find the thing you've been covering — yours, not someone else's. Confess it to one person and leave it. Then stop running. There's nobody behind you but you.

---

*Note on translation: No yirah in this chapter. 28:14 ashrei adam m'fached tamid — "happy is the man that feareth alway" (KJV) — the verb is pachad (dread), NOT yirah; there is no divine object; the antithesis is maqsheh libbo (hardens his heart, Pharaoh's verb, Exod 7:3) — the verse commends vigilant dread of one's own capacity to fall, not fear of God; a false "awe" correction here would be wrong, and the traditional "fear God" reading imports an object that isn't there. Paired deliberately with 28:13. 28:1 nasu v'ein-rodef = they flee with no pursuer; kikfir yivtach = confident as a young lion. 28:3 gever rash — MT "poor man"; some emend to rosh "ruler/head" — MT retained (the poor oppressing the poor). 28:4, 7, 9 torah = teaching/instruction, not "law." 28:8 b'neshech v'tarbit = by bite (interest) and increase — usury vocabulary (Lev 25:36–37). 28:9 to'evah — prayer filed as detestable (15:8, 21:27). 28:11 yachq'rennu = searches him out. 28:12, 28:28 y'chuppas adam = a man is searched for — people go into hiding; bookends. 28:13 m'chasseh f'sha'av = covers his transgressions (contrast 10:12, 17:9 — covering another's); modeh v'ozev = confesses and forsakes; y'rucham = is shown compassion (racham, Exod 34:6) → Ps 32:3–5, 1 John 1:9. 28:17 ad-bor yanus = flees to the pit — the grave, not hell. 28:19 sova-rish = full of poverty. 28:22 ra-ayin = evil of eye (23:6). 28:23 acharai = afterward. 28:24 ein-pasha = "no transgression" — self-justification. 28:25 r'chav-nefesh = wide of soul/appetite. 28:26 boteach b'libbo hu k'sil = one trusting in his own heart is a fool (cf. 4:23 guard vs. trust; Jer 17:9); yimmalet = escapes.*
