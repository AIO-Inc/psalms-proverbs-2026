# PROVERB 30 — I Am Too Stupid to Be a Man

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Proverbs 30:1–33. "The words of Agur son of Jakeh" — an outsider, possibly a non-Israelite from Massa (an Ishmaelite tribe, Gen 25:14), and the only voice in the book that opens by confessing it doesn't know anything. Then a prayer (30:7–9), and a run of numerical sayings — the "two things," the "four that are never satisfied," the "four small but wise," the "four stately in their walk." Every verse present. No yirah verse. Companion: Ps 131; Ps 8; Job 38–42; Prov 6:6 (the ant); Prov 8 (Wisdom at creation).*

---

Solomon's done. The king's sayings are over. And the scribes hand the mic to a man nobody's heard of, who opens by saying he's an idiot.

**"The words of Agur son of Jakeh, from Massa. The man's oracle: 'I am weary, O God; I am weary, O God, and worn out.'"**

The Hebrew of that first line is so rough that the old Bibles turned the words into names — "unto Ithiel, even unto Ithiel and Ucal." But l'iti'el can be read as la'iti El — "I am weary, God" — and ukhal as va-ekhel, "and I am spent." So the outsider doesn't open with a proverb. He opens with exhaustion. And then:

**"Surely I am too stupid to be a man; I don't have human understanding. I haven't learned wisdom, and I have no knowledge of the Holy One."**

Ba'ar anochi me-ish. I am more brutish than a man — chapter twelve's ba'ar, the ox-word. Twenty-nine chapters of the wisest man in Israel, and then a foreigner says: I'm dumber than the average person. And the scribes kept it. Because this is what the awe the whole book has been about sounds like from inside a human chest. Psalm 131 — "I don't concern myself with things too great for me." Job, after the whirlwind — "I spoke of things I didn't understand." Agur is the book's Job. The beginning of wisdom is awe, chapter one said; awe feels like this.

Verse four, and he's looking up: "Who has gone up to heaven and come down? Who has gathered the wind in his fists? Who has wrapped the waters in a cloak? Who has established all the ends of the earth? What is his name, and what is his son's name — surely you know!"

Job 38 — "where were you when I laid the foundations?" Chapter eight — Wisdom was there before the first dust. Agur asks the same questions and doesn't claim to have been in the room. Mah-sh'mo u-mah-shem-b'no — what's his name, and his son's name. Christians have heard the Son in that line for two thousand years; the plain sense is Agur demanding the credentials of anyone who claims to know how the world was built. "Tell me his name — you seem so sure." That's the sarcasm of a humble man at a confident one.

Verses five and six: **"Every word of God is refined; He is a shield to those who take refuge in Him. Don't add to His words, or He'll rebuke you and prove you a liar."** Tz'rufah — refined, the furnace-word, chapter seventeen, twenty-seven — God's words have been through the fire. Yours haven't. So al-tosef — don't add. Deuteronomy 4. The man who says he knows nothing is the one qualified to say: stop adding to what God said. Psalm 18:30 — "the word of God is refined; He is a shield." Agur is quoting David back at Solomon's heirs.

The prayer — verses seven through nine. The only prayer in Proverbs.

**"Two things I ask of You; don't refuse me before I die: Remove falsehood and lying far from me. Give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me the bread that is my portion — or I'll be full and deny You and say, 'Who is God?' Or I'll be poor and steal, and profane the name of my God."**

Lechem chuqqi — the bread of my allotment. My ration. That's give us this day our daily bread — Jesus prayed Agur's prayer. And look at the logic: too much, and I'll forget You exist. Too little, and I'll steal and drag Your name through it. He's not asking for comfort. He's asking for the amount that keeps him honest. Chapter fifteen — better a little with awe. Chapter thirty — exactly enough, please, for the sake of my honesty. That's a prayer almost nobody prays and everybody should.

Verse ten and the generation. Verse ten: **"Don't slander a servant to his master, or he'll curse you and you'll pay for it."** Even the slave has a defender. Then verses eleven through fourteen, four lines each beginning dor — "a generation": "A generation that curses its father and doesn't bless its mother. A generation pure in its own eyes, yet not washed of its filth. A generation — how lofty its eyes, how lifted its eyelids! A generation whose teeth are swords and whose jaws are knives, to devour the poor from the earth and the needy from among men." Four generations, or one seen four ways: contempt for parents, self-righteousness, arrogance, and teeth that eat the poor. Agur doesn't finish the sentence — there's no verb, no "will be punished." He just describes the generation and stops. You're supposed to recognize it.

The numerical sayings. This is Agur's signature — chapter six's "six things, seven," now as a style. Verses fifteen and sixteen: **"The leech has two daughters: 'Give! Give!' Three things are never satisfied — four never say 'Enough!': Sheol, the barren womb, the land never satisfied with water, and fire that never says 'Enough!'"** Hav hav — give, give. The leech's daughters. Then the four that never say hon. The grave — chapter twenty-seven, never satisfied. The empty womb — Rachel, Hannah, give me children or I die. Dry ground. Fire. Agur's putting the barren woman's ache next to the grave's, and he's not judging it. He's saying: some hungers are structural. They don't stop. Know which of yours is one.

Verse seventeen: **"The eye that mocks a father and scorns obedience to a mother — the ravens of the valley will pluck it out; the young eagles will eat it."** The eye that looked down on the parents, eaten by birds. Brutal, and it's about the eye — the contempt, the look.

Verses eighteen and nineteen, the best one: "Three things are too wonderful for me — four I don't understand: the way of an eagle in the sky, the way of a serpent on a rock, the way of a ship in the heart of the sea, and the way of a man with a young woman."

Nifl'u mimenni — too wonderful for me — Psalm 139's word, Psalm 131's. Four ways, derech, four paths that leave no trace and can't be explained: the eagle's route through air, the snake's over stone, the ship's through water — and derech gever b'almah, the way of a man with a woman. Agur puts falling in love on the list of things that are past understanding, right next to how an eagle finds its way. Not "how a man conquers." The way. The mystery of it. Song of Songs in one line, from the man who says he's too stupid to be human. He understands exactly one thing: that some things are nifla'ot, and you stand in awe of them instead of explaining them.

Verse twenty, right after, the anti-portrait: "This is the way of the adulteress: she eats and wipes her mouth and says, 'I've done nothing wrong.'" Lo-fa'alti aven. Chapter twenty-eight's "it's not wrong." Same derech word, ruined. The wonder of verse nineteen, reduced to a meal and a napkin.

Verses twenty-one through twenty-three: **"Under three things the earth trembles — under four it can't bear up: a slave who becomes king, a fool who is full of bread, an unloved woman who gets a husband, and a maidservant who displaces her mistress."** Four things out of place — power to the unready, food to the kesil, and two household upheavals. Agur isn't defending the class system. He's saying sudden reversals shake the ground, whoever they happen to.

Verses twenty-four through twenty-eight, the four small and wise: "Four things on earth are small, yet they are exceedingly wise: the ants — a people without strength, yet they store their food in summer; the rock badgers — a people without power, yet they make their homes in the cliffs; the locusts — they have no king, yet all of them march in rank; the lizard — you can catch it in your hands, yet it's in kings' palaces." Am lo-az — a people not strong. Chapter six's ant, now in a set of four. Every one of them is weak and wise, and the wisdom is knowing what you are — the ant stores, the badger hides in rock, the locust moves together, the lizard is in the palace because nobody bothers to stop something that small. Agur, the man who called himself brutish, loves the small wise things. He's one of them.

Verses twenty-nine through thirty-one: **"Three things are stately in their stride — four stately in their walk: the lion, mightiest among beasts, who turns back from nothing; the strutting rooster; the he-goat; and a king with his army around him."** Chapter twenty-eight — bold as a lion. And a rooster. Agur puts the chicken on the list of majestic walkers, on purpose, so you'd laugh and then look at the king differently.

And verses thirty-two and thirty-three, the last: "If you've been foolish in exalting yourself, or if you've been scheming — hand over mouth! For pressing milk produces curd, pressing the nose produces blood, and pressing anger produces strife."

Yad l'feh — hand to mouth. Shut it. The whole chapter's advice, from the man who opened by saying he knew nothing: if you've been lifting yourself up — nasa, the carrying-word — put your hand over your mouth. Chapter seventeen — even a fool who's silent passes for wise. And then the last picture, three pressings with the same word mitz: churn milk, get curd; squeeze a nose, get blood; squeeze af — nostril, anger — get a fight. Stop squeezing. That's how Agur ends. Not with a proverb about God. With: take your hand off the nose.

Here's the shape. The outsider opens exhausted and says he's dumber than a man — and that is the awe the book's been teaching. Who gathered the wind in his fist? Tell me his name — you seem sure. God's words are refined; don't add to them. Give me exactly enough bread to stay honest. Four things never say "enough," and the barren womb is one of them. Four ways too wonderful to understand, and a man with a woman is one of them. Small, weak, and wise. And when you've been puffing yourself up — hand over mouth; stop squeezing the nose.

Today: pray Agur's prayer, out loud — not too much, not too little; the ration that keeps me honest. Then find your one nifla'ot — the thing you can't explain and shouldn't try to — and stand there a minute.

*Note on translation: No yirah in this chapter. 30:1 ha-massa — "the prophecy" (KJV) — better read as the toponym Massa (Gen 25:14, an Ishmaelite tribe), making Agur a non-Israelite; l'iti'el l'iti'el v'ukhal — KJV "unto Ithiel, even unto Ithiel and Ucal" (as names) — widely repointed as la'iti El, la'iti El va-ekhel = "I am weary, God, I am weary, God, and spent" — both readings noted; the weariness reading followed. 30:2 ba'ar anochi me-ish = I am more brutish than a man (12:1 ba'ar). 30:3 da'at q'doshim = knowledge of the Holy One (9:10). 30:4 cf. Job 38:4–11, Ps 8, Prov 8:22–31; mah-shem-b'no — the Christological reading is noted as reception history; plain sense = a challenge to anyone claiming cosmic knowledge. 30:5 tz'rufah = refined (Ps 18:30, 12:6); 30:6 al-tosef (Deut 4:2, 12:32). 30:8 lechem chuqqi = bread of my portion/allotment → Matt 6:11 epiousios; mi YHWH = "Who is God?" — the denial of the sated. 30:11–14 dor ×4 — a description without a main verb; rendered as a catalog. 30:15 aluqah = leech; hav hav = give, give. 30:16 Sheol — "the grave" (KJV here, correctly); otzer racham = a closed/barren womb — placed among structural hungers without judgment. 30:17 liqhat-em — "obedience to a mother"; some emend to "the old age of a mother." 30:18–19 nifl'u mimenni = too wonderful for me (Ps 139:6, 131:1); derech gever b'almah = the way of a man with a young woman — almah = young woman of marriageable age (Isa 7:14); the mystery, not conquest, is the subject. 30:20 lo-fa'alti aven = I have done no wrong (cf. 28:24). 30:26 sh'fannim = rock badgers/hyrax (KJV "conies"). 30:28 s'mamit = lizard (KJV "spider") — lizard is the majority modern reading. 30:31 zarzir motnayim — "greyhound" (KJV) — literally "girt of loins"; rooster is the majority reading (LXX alektor); melech alqum immo — "a king against whom there is no rising up" (KJV) or "a king with his army/people with him" — disputed; the latter followed. 30:32 yad l'feh = hand to mouth; nasa = lift up (self-exaltation). 30:33 mitz ×3 = pressing/squeezing; appayim = nostrils/anger — the pun is in the Hebrew.*
