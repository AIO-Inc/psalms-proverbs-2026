# PROVERB 1 — Awe Is Where It Starts

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Proverbs 1:1–33. Opening of the book: Solomon's title, the purpose statement, the thesis verse (1:7), the first father-to-son lecture, and Lady Wisdom's first speech in the street. Companion text: Psalm 111:10 → 112 — the same thesis, the same correction.*

---

You finished the Psalms. Those were songs — you sang them up at God. Proverbs is the other direction. This is God's people handing wisdom *down* — a father sitting his kid at the kitchen table and saying, "Listen. Here's how life actually works."

**"The proverbs of Solomon, son of David, king of Israel."** That's the label on the box. Solomon — the man who asked for wisdom instead of money and got both. Whether every line is his pen or his school, the tradition put his name on it because he's the proof of concept.

Then the purpose statement, verses two through six. Why does this book exist? **To know wisdom and training.** To understand words that have insight in them. To receive the training that makes you good at doing right, at justice, at fairness. To give the naive some street-smarts and the young some knowledge and a plan.

Notice the word "training." The old Bibles say "instruction," and the Hebrew is *musar* — and *musar* is not punishment. It's what a coach does. It's discipline the way an athlete means it — the shaping, the reps, the correction that makes you better. Hold onto that, because this book uses it constantly and the punishment reading has done real damage. We'll deal with that when we hit the rod verses.

Verse five: **"Let the wise one hear and add to his learning."** So this isn't just for beginners. The book says the wise get wiser by listening. The dull one is the one who thinks he's done.

Then verse seven. Stop. This is the whole book in one line, and it's the most mistranslated line in it.

**"The AWE of God is the beginning of knowledge; fools despise wisdom and training."**

Every English Bible on your shelf says "the *fear* of the LORD." And you've probably heard it preached that way — that wisdom starts with being scared of God. It doesn't. The Hebrew word is *yirah*, and *yirah* is what happens in your chest when you stand at the edge of the Grand Canyon. It's awe. It's being so struck by the size and goodness of something that you get quiet and pay attention.

You already heard this exact line in Psalm 111:10 — "the awe of God is the beginning of wisdom" — and then Psalm 112 painted the person who has it: gracious, compassionate, generous, not shaken. That person isn't terrified. That person is *steady*. Awe makes you teachable. Fear makes you hide. God wants the first one.

And the other half: **fools despise wisdom and training.** The word for "fool" here is *evil* — not the English word evil, but the Hebrew for thick-headed. Stubborn. The one who hears good counsel and rolls his eyes. Proverbs has three grades of fool and you'll meet them all: the *peti*, who's naive and can still learn; the *kesil*, who's dull and doesn't want to; and the *letz*, the mocker, who's made a lifestyle out of sneering. Keep track. The book is trying to make sure you're none of them.

Now the father speaks. Verse eight: **"Hear, my son, your father's training, and don't abandon your mother's teaching."** Both parents. The mother's *torah* — her teaching — gets equal billing. And then: they'll be a garland on your head and a chain around your neck. The wisdom your parents gave you isn't a cage. It's jewelry. It makes you look good.

Then the first warning, verses ten through nineteen, and it's about the crew. **"My son, if the mark-missers try to recruit you, don't go."** Here's their pitch, and notice how honest the father is about how attractive it sounds: "Come with us. Let's set an ambush. Let's take somebody down for no reason. We'll swallow them alive. We'll get all their stuff, fill our houses with loot. Throw in with us — one wallet, all of us."

That's the gang. That's the hustle. That's the deal that always shows up around age sixteen, and again at twenty-five, and again at forty in a nicer suit. Easy money, shared risk, a brotherhood. The father doesn't pretend it's not appealing.

He just tells you the ending. **"Their feet run to evil, they hurry to shed blood."** And then the line that should be tattooed on everybody who ever thought about it: **"In vain the net is spread in the sight of any bird."** Meaning — a bird that *sees* the net doesn't fly into it. But these guys? **"They lie in wait for their own blood; they ambush their own lives."** You heard this in Psalm 7 and Psalm 9 and Psalm 35 — the pit-digger falls in his own pit. The trap you set for someone else has your name on it. Verse nineteenth: **"That's how it goes for everybody greedy for gain — it takes the life of the one who owns it."**

Then something amazing happens. Verse twenty. A woman stands up in the street.

**"Wisdom cries out in the street; in the plazas she lifts her voice."** This is Lady Wisdom. Proverbs personifies wisdom as a woman — and not a quiet one. She's on the corner. She's at the city gate where the business gets done. She's *loud*. And she's saying: **"How long, you naive ones, will you love being naive? How long will mockers love mocking and dull ones hate knowledge?"**

You hear that "how long"? That's a Psalm word. Psalm 13, Psalm 62 — how long, God? Now God's wisdom says it back to us. How long are *you* going to keep this up?

**"Turn back at my correction,"** she says — verse twenty-three — **"and I'll pour out my spirit on you. I'll make my words known to you."** That's the offer. Turn, and she'll flood you. That's the *tochachat*, the correction, and it's a gift with a hand open.

But she's honest about the alternative. Verses twenty-four through thirty-two: "I called, you refused. I reached out my hand, nobody paid attention. You ignored all my advice and wanted none of my correction. So when trouble comes like a storm — and it will — I'll laugh. You'll call, I won't answer. You'll look for me and not find me." Why? **"Because they hated knowledge and did not choose the awe of God."**

There's the word again, verse twenty-nine. *Yirah*. Awe. And look at the verb: **they did not *choose* it.** Awe is a choice. It's not something that happens to you when God scares you. It's a posture you decide to take — the posture of the one who stands still and pays attention. They chose not to.

And the consequence isn't God zapping them. Verse thirty-one: **"They will eat the fruit of their own way and be filled with their own schemes."** That's the pit-digger again. The punishment for ignoring wisdom is *getting what you wanted*. **"The complacency of dull ones destroys them."**

But she doesn't end there. Verse thirty-three, last word, and it's a door: **"But whoever listens to me will dwell secure, and be at ease, without dread of harm."**

Listen — that last word is *pachad*. That's real fear. Dread. The Hebrew uses two different words in this one chapter and English flattened them both to "fear." Awe of God at the start; freedom from dread at the end. Those aren't the same thing. They're opposites. The one who chooses awe gets to *stop* being afraid.

Here's the shape. Awe is where it starts — not fear, awe. Your parents' wisdom is jewelry, not a leash. The crew that promises easy money is digging your grave with your own hands. And Wisdom is standing on the corner right now, shouting, with her hand out.

Today: pick one place where you've been the naive one, the one who "loves being naive." Turn around. Choose awe. Let her pour.

*Note on translation: 1:7 and 1:29 —* yirat YHWH*, "the fear of the LORD" in KJV/ESV/NIV — rendered "AWE of God."* Yirah *is reverent awe, not dread; this is the thesis verse of the whole book and the same correction made at Psalm 111:10 (see the 111→112 pairing). 1:29 adds the verb* bacharu *— they did not* choose *awe — which makes the posture volitional, not an emotional reaction to threat. 1:33* pachad *("dread of harm") is genuine human fear and is rendered as such; the same English word "fear" for both* yirah *and* pachad *in most translations erases a distinction the chapter is built on. 1:2, 1:3, 1:7, 1:8* musar *— "instruction" in KJV — rendered "training/discipline" in the athletic sense, not punishment. 1:7* evilim *= thick-headed/stubborn fools (not the English "evil"); 1:22 introduces all three fool-grades:* peta'im *(naive),* letzim *(mockers),* kesilim *(dull). 1:8* torat immecha*, "your mother's teaching," not "law." 1:10* chatta'im *= mark-missers. 1:23* tochachat *= correction. 1:17 the bird/net proverb is elliptical in Hebrew; rendered as the tradition reads it — the bird that sees the net escapes; the greedy don't. 1:20* chochmot *is a plural/intensive form for personified Wisdom. Yirah in this chapter: 1:7, 1:29. A note on the divine name, for the whole book:* YHWH *(the personal name, hidden behind "LORD" in the KJV) and* Elohim *(the generic "God") are both rendered "God" for the ear; every* yirat YHWH *correction below is the personal name.*
