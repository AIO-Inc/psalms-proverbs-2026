# PSALM 137 — By the Rivers of Babylon

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Psalm 137:1–9. Nine verses. No author named — written in or just after the Babylonian exile, 586 BC and following. The most beautiful opening in the Psalter and the most brutal closing. Both are in the same poem on purpose. Imprecatory — handle as a container, not a command.*

---

Psalm 136 — twenty-six times *His loyal-love endures forever*. Psalm 137 — the people who sang that are sitting in the dirt by a foreign river, and someone's asking them to sing it *again*. For entertainment.

**"By the rivers of Babylon — we sat and wept — when we remembered Zion."**

*The rivers of Babylon.* The Tigris. The Euphrates. The irrigation canals between them. Babylon was a *water* city — flat, green, canal-crossed. Nothing like the hill country they came from. And the exiles — Psalm 42 said *my soul thirsts* — are surrounded by water and *sat down and wept*.

*When we remembered Zion.* Psalm 132 — *remember David*. Psalm 136 — *He remembered us*. Psalm 137 — *WE remembered*. And the memory *hurt*. There's a kind of remembering that heals and a kind that opens the wound. This is the second kind. And the psalm doesn't tell them to stop.

**"There on the poplars — we hung up our harps."**

*Hung up our harps.* The Levites — the *musicians*. The ones who'd sung 135 and 136 in the Temple courts. They brought their instruments into exile — and then *hung them in the trees*. Not smashed. *Hung.* Like you'd hang a coat you don't expect to wear again but can't throw out.

Have you ever hung up your harp? Stopped doing the thing you were *made* for because the place you did it in is gone? That's verse two.

**"For there our captors asked us for songs — our tormentors demanded songs of joy. They said: 'Sing us one of the songs of Zion!'"**

*Sing us one of the songs of Zion.* The Babylonians want a *show*. *Hey — you're the famous Temple singers. Do the one about your God being greater than all gods.* Psalm 135 — *I know the Source is great*. Sing *that* one, while you're in chains, and we'll laugh.

**"How can we sing the songs of the Source — while in a foreign land?"**

*How can we.* Not *we won't*. *How CAN we*. The songs were *for* the Temple. For the pilgrimage. For the courts. Psalm 134 — *bless the Source, you who stand in the house*. The house is *rubble*. The songs don't *work* here.

If you've ever tried to praise in a place that felt like Babylon — a hospital room, a courtroom, the house after they left — and the words wouldn't come — you know verse four. The psalm gives you permission. Sometimes the harp stays in the tree.

But — watch the turn. They won't sing for Babylon. They *will* swear an oath to Zion:

**"If I forget you, Jerusalem — may my right hand forget its skill. May my tongue cling to the roof of my mouth — if I do not remember you — if I do not consider Jerusalem my highest joy."**

*If I forget you.* The whole psalm is about *memory*. Babylon wants them to *perform*. They refuse — and instead vow to *remember*. *May my right hand forget* — the harp hand. *May my tongue stick* — the singing tongue. *If I stop remembering — take the music from me entirely.* They'd rather lose the gift than lose the *home*.

*My highest joy.* *Rosh simchati.* The *head* of my joy. Everything else is downstream.

That's the line to carry. What's your Jerusalem? The thing you'd rather lose your hands than forget? Say it. The exiles said it out loud in enemy territory.

Now — the hard part. The psalm turns from grief to *rage*. And it doesn't apologize.

**"Remember, Source — what the Edomites did — on the day Jerusalem fell. 'Tear it down!' they cried. 'Tear it down to its foundations!'"**

*Edom.* Not Babylon. *Edom.* Israel's *brother* — Esau's descendants. Genesis 25. And when Babylon came — Edom *cheered*. Obadiah 10–14 — *you stood at the crossroads and cut down the fugitives*. Lamentations 4 — *rejoice, Edom — the cup will pass to you too*. Psalm 133 — *how good when brothers dwell together*. Psalm 137 — *and here's what happens when a brother stands at the gate and yells TEAR IT DOWN.*

The betrayal by the enemy hurts. The betrayal by the *brother* — that's what's still in the throat.

**"Daughter Babylon — doomed to destruction — happy is the one who repays you — according to what you have done to us."**

*Happy.* *Ashrei.* Psalm 1's word — *happy is the one who walks in instruction*. Here — *happy is the one who pays you back*. That's a shocking use of the word. And it's honest. The exiles want Babylon to *get what she gave*. Isaiah 13, Jeremiah 50–51 — the prophets said the same. *You did it. It's coming back.*

And then — the verse. The one people skip. The one that gets cut out of the hymnal:

**"Happy is the one who seizes your infants — and dashes them against the rocks."**

Read it. Don't flinch yet. Read it and understand *what they saw*.

Because that's the thing — *this is what Babylon did to them*. 2 Kings 25. Lamentations 2 — *infants and babies faint in the streets*. Lamentations 4 — *the hands of compassionate women have cooked their own children*. Isaiah 13:16 — the prophecy against Babylon: *their infants will be dashed to pieces before their eyes*. The exiles *watched* this. In Jerusalem. To *their* children. Verse nine isn't a fantasy they invented. It's a *memory* they're handing back.

So what do you do with it?

Here's what you *don't* do. You don't pretend it's not there. And you don't turn it into a command. Nobody in this psalm *does* anything to anyone. They *say* it. To *God*. That's the whole point of an imprecatory psalm — Psalm 58, 59, 69, 109 — the rage goes *up*, not *out*. It gets *said* so it doesn't get *done*. The psalm is a *container*. You pour the thing that would destroy you — or someone else — into words, and you hand the words to the only One strong enough to hold them.

Because here's the alternative. The exile who *doesn't* say verse nine — who swallows it, buries it, pretends he's fine — that's the exile who does something with his hands twenty years later. Or dies with it inside him. The Bible kept this verse because *unspoken rage is more dangerous than spoken rage*. The tradition let them scream. In the text. Where God could hear it and *they* couldn't act on it.

And the Hebrew word for the infants — *olalim* — is the *same word* Lamentations uses for *their* infants. The exiles are saying: *You want to know what happy looks like? Someone doing to you exactly what you did to us.* It's the law of *measure for measure*. It's Psalm 7 — *the pit-digger falls in his own pit*. It's ugly because what happened was ugly.

Did God do it? Babylon fell — 539 BC, to Persia. Cyrus took the city *without* a massacre. The infants of Babylon weren't dashed on rocks. The rage was *heard*. It wasn't *enacted*. That's what a container does.

Nine verses. Here's the shape: *By the rivers — we sat and wept. Hung our harps in the trees. They wanted a show — how can we sing here? But — if I forget you, Jerusalem, take my hands, take my tongue. — And Source: remember Edom, the brother who cheered. Babylon — what you did comes back. Even THAT. Even the children.* And then — silence. No verse ten. No resolution. It just *stops*.

Here's what to do with your Babylon. Whatever was done to you — whoever cheered while it happened — write verse nine. *Your* verse nine. The worst thing you want to happen to them. Say it *to God*. Out loud. Then leave it there. Don't send the text. Don't make the call. *Hang it in the tree with the harp.* You're not being holy by pretending you don't feel it. You're being holy by *giving it to the only One who can hold it without breaking*.

The psalm that begins with the most beautiful line in the Bible ends with the ugliest. Same people. Same day. That's what exile does. And the canon kept both — so you'd know you're allowed to bring *both* to the river.

*Note on translation: v. 1 — *al naharot Bavel* = "by the rivers of Babylon" — the Euphrates/Tigris and the canal system; *sham yashavnu gam-bachinu* = "there we sat, also we wept." v. 2 — *aravim* = poplars/willows (Populus euphratica); *talinu kinnoroteinu* = "we hung our lyres." v. 3 — *shovenu* = our captors; *tolalenu* = our tormentors (uncertain; possibly "those who made us wail"). v. 4 — *shir-YHWH* = "song of the Source" — the Temple repertoire. v. 5–6 — self-curse formula; *tishkach y'mini* = "may my right hand forget" — some emend to "wither"; *rosh simchati* = "the head/chief of my joy." v. 7 — *b'nei Edom* — Edom's complicity: Obad 10–14; Lam 4:21–22; Ezek 25:12–14; 35:5. v. 8 — *bat-Bavel ha-sh'dudah* = "daughter Babylon, the devastated one" (participle — some read as prophetic perfect: "the one to be devastated"); *ashrei* = happy — same word as 1:1, deliberately jarring. v. 9 — *olalayich* = your infants — same term as Lam 2:11, 19; 4:4 for Jerusalem's slaughtered children; *el-ha-sala* = against the rock — cf. Isa 13:16; 2 Kgs 8:12; Hos 13:16; Nah 3:10 — standard ancient siege atrocity, here returned as *lex talionis*. **Imprecatory framing: the violence is spoken to God, not enacted; historically Babylon fell to Cyrus (539 BC) without massacre. Render at full strength; frame as container, never as directive.** Not quoted in NT; Rev 18 echoes the fall-of-Babylon motif. No *yirah* in this psalm.*
