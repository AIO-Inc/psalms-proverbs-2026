# PSALM 45 — The Wedding Song

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Psalm 45:1–17. A royal wedding song — the king and his bride. Hebrews 1:8–9 applies it directly to Christ.*

---

After 44's belly-in-the-dirt — a wedding. The book does that. Grief, then a party. Because that's the real order of things.

**"My heart is overflowing with a good word. I'm speaking of my work for the king. My tongue is the pen of a skilled writer."**

*Overflowing.* The Hebrew is *rachash* — to bubble, to boil over. The poet can't hold it in. He's got a good thing to say and it's coming out whether he's ready or not.

**Part One: The Groom.**

"You are the most handsome of the sons of Adam. Grace is poured on your lips — because God has blessed you forever."

*Grace on your lips.* Not just a handsome face — a mouth that *gives*. That's the first thing said about this king. Not his sword. His *words*.

"Strap your sword on your thigh, mighty one — your splendor and your majesty. In your majesty ride out victoriously — **for the cause of truth, humility, and right-standing.**"

He does have a sword. But look what it's *for*. Not conquest. *Truth. Humility. Right-standing.* A warrior for gentleness. That's the paradox at the heart of this king.

"Let your right hand teach you awesome deeds. Your arrows are sharp. Nations fall under you. The king's enemies lose heart."

And then — the verse Hebrews quotes:

**"Your throne, God, is forever and ever. The scepter of your kingdom is a scepter of uprightness.**
**You've loved right-standing and hated wickedness. So God — your God — has anointed you with the oil of joy above your companions."**

*Your throne, God.* The poet calls the king *God*. Hebrews 1:8 says: *this is what the Father says to the Son.* The royal wedding song was pointing at a Wedding nobody at the ceremony could see yet.

*The oil of joy.* Not the oil of power. Not the oil of authority. *Joy.* He's anointed with *gladness* — above everyone else in the room. The King's defining quality, according to this psalm, is that He's the happiest one there.

"Your robes are all myrrh and aloes and cassia. From ivory palaces, stringed music makes you glad. Daughters of kings are among your honored ladies. **At your right hand stands the queen — in gold of Ophir.**"

**Part Two: The Bride.**

Now the poet turns and speaks to *her*. And here's the counsel he gives:

**"Listen, daughter. Look. Turn your ear. Forget your people and your father's house. The king desires your beauty — because he is your lord, bow to him."**

*Forget your father's house.* This is the hard line. She's leaving. Everything she was — the old home, the old name, the old loyalties. She has to let go of the past to step into this.

If you've ever left an old life for a new one — the substance, the crowd, the identity — you know exactly what "forget your father's house" costs. And you know it's the price of the wedding.

*The king desires your beauty.* Not "tolerates." *Desires.* She's wanted. Whatever she's leaving — she's walking toward someone who *wants her there*.

"The daughter of Tyre will come with a gift. The rich among the people will seek your favor. **All glorious is the king's daughter inside — her clothing is woven with gold.**"

*Glorious inside.* Literally *within* — inside the palace, and inside herself. The gold on the outside matches something on the inside.

**"In embroidered robes she's led to the king. Her virgin companions follow her — they're brought to you. They're led with joy and gladness. They enter the palace of the king."**

*Led with joy.* The whole procession — not one anxious face. Everyone's glad. The bride, the bridesmaids, the crowd. It's a *happy* wedding.

**Part Three: The Future.**

"In place of your fathers will be your sons. You'll make them princes in all the earth."

*In place of your fathers.* The bride was told to forget her father's house. Now — a new house. Sons. Princes. The old family let go; a new dynasty starts.

**"I will make your name remembered in all generations. So the peoples will praise you forever and ever."**

That's the poet's promise. *I'm writing this down so no one forgets.* And here we are, three thousand years later, reading it.

So: your heart overflowing with a good word — say it. The King is anointed with *joy*, and His sword is for *humility*. Forget your father's house — the one you're walking toward *desires* you. And the procession is glad, all the way in.
