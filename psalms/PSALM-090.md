# PSALM 90 — Teach Us to Number Our Days

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Psalm 90:1–17. Attributed to Moses — the oldest psalm in the book. Book Four opens with it. The answer to Psalm 89's "where is the promise?" is: God was home before there was a promise.*

---

Psalm 89 ended with the throne in the dirt. Book Four's editors answered with the oldest voice they had — *Moses*. Before David. Before kings. Before the temple. A man who spent forty years in a wilderness watching an entire generation die.

This is what he learned.

**"Master — You have been our dwelling place throughout all generations."**

*Dwelling place.* Not the temple — that didn't exist. Not the land — they hadn't entered it. *You.* God as the *home*. Before there was a home. After the home burned. *You've been the address.*

**"Before the mountains were born — before You brought forth the whole world — from everlasting to everlasting — You are God."**

*Before the mountains were born.* Mountains are the oldest thing anyone can see. And God's *older*. Psalm 89 was worried the promise was too old to still count. Moses says: *He's older than the mountains and He's still here.*

Now — the hard part. Moses on mortality:

**"You turn people back to dust — saying: 'Return to dust, you mortals.'"**

*Return to dust.* Genesis 3. Moses is quoting the curse. He watched it happen to a whole generation — the ones who wouldn't go into the land.

**"A thousand years in Your sight are like a day that has just gone by — or like a watch in the night."**

*A thousand years — one day.* Or less — *a watch in the night*. Three or four hours. That's a millennium to God. Which means the six hundred years the throne would be empty — *an afternoon*.

**"Yet You sweep people away in the sleep of death. They are like the new grass of the morning — though in the morning it springs up new — by evening it is dry and withered."**

*Morning grass.* Green at dawn. Brown by dinner. That's a human life against God's calendar. Not depressing — *clarifying*. You've got a day. Spend it right.

**"We are consumed by Your anger — and terrified by Your indignation. You have set our wrongdoing before You — our secret sins in the light of Your presence."**

*Secret sins in the light.* Psalm 19 — *hidden faults*. Nothing stays hidden in front of Him.

**"All our days pass away under Your wrath. We finish our years with a moan."**

*With a moan.* Moses doesn't soften it. Most lives end in a sigh, not a fanfare.

**"Our days may come to seventy years — or eighty, if our strength endures — yet the best of them are but trouble and sorrow — because they quickly pass — and we fly away."**

*Seventy. Eighty.* Moses lived to 120 — and he's telling you *you* won't. And even the *best* years — *trouble and sorrow*. He's not selling you a soft life. He's telling you the truth so you'll spend it well.

**"If only we knew the power of Your anger! Your wrath is as great as the awe that is Your due."**

*Awe.* *Yirah.* Even here — in the psalm about dying — the response to God's greatness is *awe*, not terror.

And then — the verse. The one the whole psalm was heading toward:

**"Teach us to number our days — so that we may gain a heart of wisdom."**

*Number our days.* Count them. Know how many you've got — roughly. Not to be morbid. *So that* — purpose clause — *we gain a heart of wisdom.* The person who knows they've got a limited number of days spends them differently than the person who thinks they've got forever.

*Teach us.* It has to be *taught*. It doesn't come naturally. Every human being's default setting is *I have time*. Moses is asking God to override the default.

That's the whole psalm in one line. *Count the days — get wise.*

Now — the turn. And after the dust and the grass and the seventy years, it's *tender*:

**"Relent, Source! How long will it be? Have compassion on Your servants."**

*How long.* Moses asked it too. In the wilderness. The oldest question in the book.

**"Satisfy us in the morning with Your loyal-love — that we may sing for joy and be glad all our days."**

*In the morning.* The grass springs up in the morning. Moses wants the *loyal-love* to spring up with it. *Satisfy us* — Psalm 63's fat and marrow — *first thing*. So the day that ends in a sigh can *start* in a song.

**"Make us glad for as many days as You have afflicted us — for as many years as we have seen trouble."**

*As many days.* Moses is asking for the *math to balance*. Forty years in the wilderness — forty years of gladness to match. He's not asking to erase the hard years. He's asking for *equal time on the other side*.

**"May Your deeds be shown to Your servants — Your splendor to their children."**

*To their children.* Psalm 78 — the next generation. Moses knew he wouldn't enter the land. He's praying for the *kids* who would.

And the last verse — a builder's prayer:

**"May the favor of the Master our God rest on us. Establish the work of our hands for us — yes — establish the work of our hands."**

*Establish the work of our hands.* Twice. The one thing a person with seventy years wants: *let what I built LAST.* Let it not be grass. Let it not blow away when I do. Moses built a *nation* in a wilderness — and he's asking God to make it *stick*.

That's the answer to Psalm 89. *The throne's in the dirt? He's been the dwelling place since before the mountains. A thousand years is an afternoon. Count your days. Ask Him to establish the work. Then let the kids see what comes next.*

Seventeen verses. Here's the shape: *You've been home forever. We're morning grass. Seventy years — eighty if we're lucky — and they end in a sigh. Teach us to count them so we get wise. Satisfy us first thing in the morning. Balance the hard years with glad ones. Show the kids. Make what we built last.*

Count your days. Then go build something He'll establish.
