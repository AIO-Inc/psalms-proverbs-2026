# PSALM 53 — The Fool, Second Edition

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Psalm 53:1–6. A near-duplicate of Psalm 14 — but placed in Book Two, with "God" (Elohim) replacing "the Source" (YHWH), and one verse rewritten. The differences are the message.*

---

You've read this one. Psalm 14. *The fool says in his heart, there is no God.* Same opening, same diagnosis, same "not even one." So why is it here again?

Because the editors wanted you to hear it *twice* — in a different key, with a different ending.

**"The fool says in his heart: 'There is no God.' They're corrupt. They do disgusting wrong. There is no one who does good."**

Same as before. *In his heart* — not his mouth. The practical atheism of living like nobody's watching.

**"God looks down from heaven on the children of Adam — to see if there's anyone who understands, anyone who seeks God. Every one has turned back. Together they've gone rotten. There is no one who does good — not even one."**

Same as before. The universal diagnosis. Everybody's off the road.

**"Don't they know anything — these workers of harm, who eat up My people like bread, and don't call on God?"**

Same. The casual consumption of people.

Now — here's where it changes. Psalm 14:5–6 said:

> *There they were terrified — because God is with the generation of the one who does right. You'd shame the plans of the poor — but the Source is his refuge.*

Psalm 53:5 says:

**"There — they were terrified with a terror that had never been — because God has scattered the bones of the one who camps against you. You've put them to shame — because God has rejected them."**

*A terror that had never been.* Psalm 14's fools were scared when they realized God was with the poor. Psalm 53's fools are scared because *God scattered their bones.* Something *happened*. Between the writing of 14 and the placing of 53, there was a battle — and the ones camping against God's people were *destroyed*.

Most scholars think this is Sennacherib — the Assyrian army that surrounded Jerusalem in 701 BC and woke up one morning with 185,000 dead (2 Kings 19:35). The fools who said "there is no God" were camped outside the walls with the biggest army on earth. And the bones got scattered.

So — same psalm, updated. Psalm 14 was the *warning*. Psalm 53 is the *report*. *Remember when we told you the fool would be terrified? Here's what it looked like.*

That's why it's here twice. The first time is prophecy. The second time is history.

And the ending — same as 14:

**"Oh, that rescue for Israel would come from Zion! When God brings His people back — Jacob will celebrate, Israel will be glad."**

Still asking. Still *bring us back*. Even after the bones got scattered — the prayer's the same. Because the enemy being gone isn't the same as being *home*. You can win the battle and still be in exile.

Here's what to take from a psalm you've already read: God repeats Himself on purpose. When you've heard something twice — once as a warning and once as a report — it's because you needed to know the warning came true. Somebody camped against God's people and lost. It's on the record.

The fool still says it in his heart. The bones are still scattered. And the prayer is still *bring us home*.

*Note on translation: Psalm 53 substitutes* Elohim *(God) for* YHWH *(the Source) throughout — characteristic of Book Two (Ps 42–72), the "Elohistic Psalter." v. 5 —* pachad lo-hayah pachad *= "a dread that had never been a dread" — unprecedented terror;* pachad*, correctly rendered as human dread, not* yirah*.* pizar atzmot *= scattered the bones — likely reference to 2 Kings 19:35 (Sennacherib, 701 BC). Compare Ps 14:5–6 for the earlier text.*
