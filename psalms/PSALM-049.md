# PSALM 49 — You Can't Take It With You

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Psalm 49:1–20. A wisdom psalm about money and death. Sons of Korah.*

---

The poet opens like a preacher clearing his throat:

**"Hear this, all you peoples. Listen, all who live in the world — low and high, rich and poor together."**

*Rich and poor together.* Both. Because what he's about to say applies to everyone with a pulse — and what he's about to say is about the one thing money can't fix.

"My mouth will speak wisdom. The meditation of my heart — understanding. I'll turn my ear to a proverb. I'll open my riddle on the harp."

*A riddle.* He's calling it a puzzle. Something you have to sit with. And he's going to *sing* it.

Here's the riddle:

**"Why should I be afraid in the days of evil — when the wickedness of the ones who'd trip me surrounds me? The ones who trust in their wealth — who boast in the abundance of their riches?"**

*Why should I be afraid of the rich?* That's the question. They've got the money. They've got the lawyers. They've got the leverage. So why isn't he scared?

Here's why:

**"No man can redeem his brother. He can't give God a ransom for him — the price of a life is too high. He can never pay enough — that he should live forever and not see the pit."**

*The price of a life is too high.* There is no amount. Not for your brother. Not for yourself. You can buy a lot of things — you cannot buy one more day past your appointed one. Every rich man who ever lived found this out.

**"Because he sees: wise men die. The fool and the senseless perish together — and leave their wealth to others."**

*To others.* Same as Psalm 39. You pile it up; strangers divide it.

**"Their graves are their homes forever — their dwelling place for all generations — though they named lands after themselves."**

*Named lands after themselves.* Buildings, towns, foundations, streets. The name's on the sign. The man's in the box. The sign doesn't help the man.

**"But man — for all his splendor — doesn't remain. He's like the animals that perish."**

That's the refrain — he'll say it again at the end. *Like the animals.* For all the suits and the cars and the boards he sat on. Same ending as a dog.

"This is the road of the ones who trust in themselves — and their followers who approve of their words."

*Followers who approve.* The rich fool has fans. People who quote him. That's part of the illusion — the crowd nodding makes it feel permanent.

**"Like sheep they're appointed for Sheol. Death will shepherd them."**

*Death will shepherd them.* Psalm 23 — *the Source is my shepherd.* Psalm 49 — *death is theirs.* Everyone gets a shepherd. You pick which one.

"The upright will rule over them in the morning. Their form will waste away in Sheol — far from any mansion."

*Far from any mansion.* The house they built to be remembered by — they're not in it.

And then — the turn. The poet steps out of the riddle and says something no one in the ancient world expected:

**"But God will redeem my soul from the power of Sheol. Because He will take me."**

*He will take me.* The word is *lakach* — the same word used when God *took* Enoch, when Elijah was *taken*. The rich man can't buy a ransom — *the price is too high*. But God *has one*. He *redeems*. He *takes* you.

That's the answer to the riddle. *Why aren't I afraid of the rich?* Because the one thing they can't buy — I've been given.

Now the advice:

**"Don't be afraid when a man gets rich — when the glory of his house increases. Because when he dies he takes nothing with him. His glory won't follow him down."**

*Won't follow him down.* The glory stays on the surface. He goes under alone.

"While he lived he blessed himself — 'People praise you when you do well for yourself!' — but he'll go to join his fathers, who'll never see the light again."

*He blessed himself.* Self-congratulation. He told himself he was doing great, and people agreed. And then — the dark.

And the refrain, second time:

**"Man — for all his splendor — has no understanding. He's like the animals that perish."**

First time: *doesn't remain*. Second time: *has no understanding*. The problem isn't just that he dies. It's that he never *understood*. Had every advantage and died as clueless as the livestock.

So here's the riddle solved: the rich man can't buy the one thing that matters, and *you already have it*. That's why you don't have to be afraid of him. Not because he's not powerful. Because *his* shepherd is death, and *yours* takes you home.

Don't be afraid when a man gets rich. Feel sorry for him. Then go count what *you've* got.

*Note on translation: v. 5 —* lamah ira *= "why should I fear" — ordinary human dread, correctly rendered (not* yirah*). v. 8 —* yekar pidyon nafsham *= "costly is the ransom of their soul." v. 14 —* mavet yir'em *= "death will shepherd them" — deliberate inversion of Ps 23:1. "Sheol" throughout = the grave, not hell. v. 15 —* yikacheni *= "He will take me" — cf. Gen 5:24 (Enoch), 2 Kings 2:3 (Elijah). v. 12, 20 — refrain;* nimshal ka-b'hemot *= "like the beasts."*
