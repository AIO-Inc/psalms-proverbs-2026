# PSALM 71 — Even When I'm Old and Gray

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Psalm 71:1–24. No author named. An old man looking back — and forward.*

---

This is the psalm for the second half of life. The writer's old. He says so. And he's not done.

**"In You, Source, I take refuge. Don't let me be ashamed — ever. In Your right-standing — rescue me and free me. Turn Your ear to me and save me."**

Psalm 31's opening, almost word for word. He's *borrowing* — because when you're old, you've got a library of prayers that worked, and you reach for them.

**"Be my rock of refuge — where I can always go. You've given the command to save me — because You're my rock and my fortress."**

*Where I can always go.* The Hebrew is *lavo tamid* — to come continually. Not an emergency exit. A *home address*. The place he's been going for decades.

"Rescue me, my God, from the hand of the wicked — from the grip of the unjust and the cruel."

And then — the retrospective:

**"Because You are my hope, Master — Source — my confidence from my youth."**

*From my youth.* He's tracing it back. Not "I found You late." *You've been there since I was a kid.*

**"On You I've leaned from birth. You brought me out of my mother's womb. My praise is continually of You."**

*From birth.* Before he could choose. Before he knew the name. The leaning started before the knowing.

**"I've become like a wonder to many — but You are my strong refuge."**

*A wonder.* The Hebrew is *mofet* — a sign, a portent, something people point at. He's saying: *people look at my life and don't know what to make of it.* Maybe because of how much he's survived. Maybe because of how strange his story is. Either way — he's a *sign*. And the sign points to the refuge.

**"My mouth is filled with Your praise — with Your glory all day long."**

Now — the old-age section. Read it slow:

**"Don't cast me off in the time of old age. Don't abandon me when my strength fails."**

There it is. The fear every aging person has and doesn't say. *When I can't produce anymore — will You still want me?* When the body slows, when the mind slips, when the useful years are behind — *don't throw me away.*

He asks it out loud. Because he's watched the world throw old people away and he wants to know God isn't like the world.

"Because my enemies talk against me. The ones watching for my life plot together. They say: 'God has abandoned him. Chase him. Grab him — there's no one to rescue him.'"

*God has abandoned him.* The enemies think old age is proof God left. That the weakness is a *verdict*. He's got no army, no strength, no defenders — so they read it as *forsaken*.

**"God — don't be far from me. My God — hurry to help me."**

Psalm 70's *hurry*. Still saying it at the end of life.

"Let my accusers be ashamed and consumed. Let the ones seeking my harm be covered with disgrace and shame."

And now — the pivot. This is where the psalm stops looking back and starts *refusing to quit*:

**"But I — I will hope continually. I will add to all Your praise."**

*I will add.* He's not done praising. He's got *more* to say. Every year of survival is new material.

**"My mouth will tell of Your right-standing — Your rescue — all day long — though I don't know how to count it all."**

*I don't know how to count it all.* Psalm 40 — *too many to count*. Now it's a lifetime's worth. He's lost track of the rescues. There've been too many.

**"I'll come with the mighty deeds of the Master — Source. I'll make known Your right-standing — Yours alone."**

**"God — You've taught me from my youth. To this day I declare Your wonders."**

*To this day.* Still going.

And then — the verse the whole psalm is built around:

**"Even when I'm old and gray — God — don't abandon me — until I declare Your strength to the next generation — Your might to all who are to come."**

*Until.* Read that word. He's not asking to be kept alive to enjoy retirement. He's asking for time — *until I've told the next generation.* The old man's job isn't done. There are kids who haven't heard yet. He needs the years to *transfer* it.

That's what old age is *for*, in this psalm. Not rest. *Transmission.* Psalm 48 — *tell the next generation.* Here's the man doing it with his last strength.

**"Your right-standing, God, reaches to the heights. You've done great things — God — who is like You?"**

Now — the honest part:

**"You've shown me many bitter troubles — but You'll bring me back to life. From the depths of the earth You'll bring me up again."**

*Many bitter troubles.* He doesn't pretend the long life was easy. *You* showed them to me. And *You'll bring me up again.* From the depths. This is an old man talking about resurrection without having the word for it yet.

**"You'll increase my honor. You'll turn and comfort me."**

*Comfort.* The Hebrew is *nacham* — the Isaiah 40 word. *Comfort ye My people.* The old man gets comforted.

And then — the harp comes out:

**"So I'll praise You with the harp — for Your faithfulness, my God. I'll sing to You with the lyre — Holy One of Israel. My lips will shout for joy when I sing to You — because You've redeemed my soul."**

*My lips will shout.* Old lips. Still shouting.

**"My tongue will tell of Your right-standing all day long — because the ones seeking my harm are ashamed and disgraced."**

Twenty-four verses. Here's the shape: *You've been my refuge since the womb. I'm a sign people point at. Don't throw me away when my strength fails — the enemies say You've left. But I'll keep hoping. I've lost count of the rescues. Keep me alive until I've told the kids. You've shown me bitter things — You'll bring me up again. And I'll get the harp out one more time.*

If you're in the second half: this is yours. You're not done. You're the *transmission*. Don't put the harp away.
