# PSALM 30 — Weeping Stays the Night, Joy Comes in the Morning

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Psalm 30:1–12. Written for the dedication of the house — after a near-death illness.*

---

This is a recovery psalm. Somebody almost died and didn't. And now he's telling everybody.

"I will lift You high, Source — because You lifted me up. You didn't let my enemies celebrate over me."

*You lifted me — so I lift You.* That's the exchange. He got pulled out of the pit, so he's putting God on the mountaintop.

**"Source, my God, I cried out to You — and You healed me. You brought my soul up from Sheol. You kept me alive from going down to the pit."**

*From Sheol.* He was that close. The grave had him by the ankle. And he came back.

"Sing to the Source, you loyal ones. Give thanks to His holy name."

Now the line that's been on more sympathy cards than any other — and it deserves better than a card:

**"His anger is for a moment. His favor is for a lifetime.**
**Weeping may stay the night — but in the morning, a shout of joy."**

Read the *proportions*. A moment — a lifetime. Whatever correction you're going through, it's *a moment*. It has an end. The favor is the *whole life*. The default setting. The hard part is the exception, not the rule.

*Weeping stays the night.* The Hebrew is *yalin* — to lodge, to spend the night as a guest. Weeping *checks in*. It's a visitor. It gets a room. But it's got a checkout time — and the checkout time is *morning*.

Now here's the honest part, and it's why this psalm matters. He tells you what he was like *before*:

**"In my security I said: 'I will never be shaken.'"**

There it is. He'd gotten comfortable. Things were good, and he started thinking things being good was *him*. That he'd built it. That it was permanent.

"Source, by Your favor You'd made my mountain strong. Then You hid Your face — and I was terrified."

*You hid Your face.* That's what the illness was. Not punishment — a *reminder*. The mountain was always His. The strength was always borrowed. He'd forgotten, and the sickness un-forgot him.

Then he tells you what he prayed on the floor — and it's brilliant:

"To You, Source, I called. To my Master I pleaded for grace. **'What profit is there in my blood — in my going down to the pit? Can dust praise You? Can it tell of Your truth?'**"

*What good am I to You dead?* He's negotiating. *Dust can't sing. Dust can't testify. Keep me breathing and I'll be useful.* It's not humble. It's not polished. It's a man bargaining for his life — and God *honored it*.

You're allowed to argue. He accepts arguments.

"Hear, Source. Be gracious. Source — be my helper."

And then — the answer. And it's the best sentence in the psalm:

**"You turned my mourning into dancing. You untied my sackcloth and clothed me with joy."**

*Turned* — the word is *hafach*, to overturn, flip completely. Not "eased." *Flipped.* The mourning didn't fade into acceptance. It became its *opposite*. Dancing.

*Untied my sackcloth.* Sackcloth is what you wore when someone died — rough, scratchy, tied on. He didn't take it off himself. *God untied it.* Loosened the knot. Slid it off his shoulders. And put on joy like a robe.

**"So that my glory will sing to You and not be silent. Source, my God — I will thank You forever."**

*Not be silent.* Remember the bargain? *Dust can't praise You.* Well — he's not dust. So he's paying it off. Forever.

Here's the recipe: the night is real. The weeping gets a room. Say your argument to God — *what good am I to You in the ground?* And then wait for morning. He unties the sackcloth Himself.

---

*Note on translation: v. 3 — "Sheol" = the grave, the realm of the dead — NOT hell. He was near death, not near damnation. v. 5 —* rega *= a moment;* chayim *= life/lifetime;* yalin *= lodges overnight (as a guest);* rinnah *= a ringing shout of joy. v. 6 —* b'shalvi *= in my ease/security. v. 11 —* hafachta *= You overturned/reversed;* pitachta saki *= You loosened/untied my sackcloth. No* yirah*.*
