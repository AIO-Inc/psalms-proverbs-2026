# PSALM 41 — The Friend Who Ate My Bread

*Motivational script in today's vernacular. Source: Hebrew text, Psalm 41:1–13. The last psalm of Book One. Jesus quoted verse 9 at the Last Supper about Judas.*

---

**"Happy is the one who pays attention to the weak. In the day of trouble the Source will rescue him."**

Book One started with *Happy is the one* (Psalm 1). It ends the same way. But look what changed — Psalm 1's happy man *delights in the instruction*. Psalm 41's happy man *pays attention to the weak*. Forty psalms later, the instruction has become a person in front of you who needs help.

*Pays attention.* The Hebrew is *maskil* — to look closely, to consider, to be wise about. Not "gives to the poor." *Sees* them. Most people walk past. The happy man stops and looks.

"The Source will guard him and keep him alive. He'll be called happy in the land. You won't hand him over to the desire of his enemies. **The Source will support him on his sickbed. You'll turn his whole bed when he's ill.**"

*Turn his bed.* The Hebrew is literally *you will change his bed* — the way a nurse turns a patient, changes the sheets, makes it bearable. God as the one doing the bedside work. The man who paid attention to the weak — when *he's* weak, he gets attended to.

Then David's own confession:

**"I said: 'Source — be gracious to me. Heal my soul. Because I've missed the mark against You.'"**

*Heal my soul.* Not just the body. He knows the sickness goes deeper than the symptoms.

Now — the enemies. And the betrayal:

"My enemies speak evil of me: 'When will he die and his name perish?' When one comes to see me, he speaks lies. **His heart gathers up wickedness. He goes outside — and tells it.**"

Picture it. Someone visits the sickbed. Sits there, says kind things — and the whole time he's *collecting*. Every weakness, every slip. Then he walks out the door and *reports*. The visitor was a spy.

"All the ones who hate me whisper together against me. They plan harm for me: 'Something deadly's been poured into him. He's lying down — he won't get up again.'"

They've already written the obituary.

And then — the verse:

**"Even my close friend — the one I trusted, who ate my bread — has lifted his heel against me."**

*Who ate my bread.* In that world, sharing a meal was a covenant. You didn't betray someone whose table you'd sat at. It was the deepest bond outside of blood.

*Lifted his heel.* A kick. From the man you fed.

John 13:18 — Jesus, at the table, bread in His hand, Judas across from Him — quotes this exact line. *He who ate my bread has lifted his heel against me.* And then hands Judas the bread anyway.

If you've been kicked by someone you fed — Jesus was too. He quoted David about it. You're in a long line.

"But You, Source — be gracious to me. Raise me up — so I can pay them back."

Honest. Not pretty. He wants to get up and settle it. The psalms don't clean that up.

**"By this I know You're pleased with me: my enemy doesn't shout in triumph over me."**

*By this I know.* Not by feelings. Not by circumstances. By the fact that the ones who said *he won't get up* — were wrong.

**"You've held me up in my integrity. You've set me before Your face forever."**

*Before Your face forever.* That's where he ends up. Not "vindicated." Not "healed." *In front of the face* — the thing he's asked for in every psalm since Psalm 4. Forever.

Then — the closing of Book One. A doxology added by the editors, and every one of the five books ends with one:

**"Blessed be the Source, the God of Israel — from forever to forever. Amen and amen."**

*Amen and amen.* Twice. *It's true — and it's true.*

Forty-one psalms. Two roads. The bed wet with tears. The shepherd. The cross. The confession. The mud. And now — the friend who ate your bread and kicked you. Book One holds all of it, and closes with *amen and amen*.

Pay attention to the weak. Get your bed turned when it's your turn. Forgive the heel if you can. And stand before the face — forever.
